Appendix C Common Method Kernel and Cross-Disciplinary Bridging ​

This appendix lists methodological responsibilities that can be shared when texts interoperate. Each text retains its own authoritative definitions and way of operating. The common kernel requires no shared chapter structure, artifact count, or numbering. It does not make one text independent empirical evidence for another.

Appendix C Common method kernel and Interdisciplinary Bridging

The shared core shares methodological responsibilities without taking away each text’s authority over definitions or letting two texts manufacture independent empirical support for one another.

Original location: Chinese source lines 4805–4808; original site location: this part’s introduction (no independently numbered anchor). See the theory-v2 source for the current full text; historical research manuscripts and excerpts remain available for download and checking against the original edition.

The introduction states that interoperability across texts can share methodological responsibilities while each text retains its authoritative definitions and operational form. What is shared is how grounds and boundaries are accounted for, not a requirement that all books use the same chapter structure, number of artifacts, or numbering. [T-intro]

“Does not make one text independent empirical evidence for the other” concerns source independence: if A cites B and B cites A in return, without new observations of an object, this cannot count as two external validations. Texts interpreting one another may have interpretive value, but material status must be retained. [T-intro]

Interoperability requires at least declarations of the current text version, cited definitions, corresponding content, and differences. Similar names do not mean identical contracts; the same example may also come from the same upstream source, and changing format does not make sources independent. [T-intro]

This topic has only the user-provided Xikari original and has not obtained the authoritative full text of World Model; it can therefore explain only this book’s proposed interoperability responsibilities, without claiming to have completed a field-by-field comparison of the two books or approved the other book’s definitions. Understanding this section

C.2 Common method constraints

Methods 1–3 specify responsible model use; following the procedure does not itself establish a model’s real-world effectiveness.

Method 1 declares task, representation, horizon, environment, operations, and error together. Task specifies what must be recovered or predicted; representation specifies what the model can see; horizon and environment limit the valid domain; operations specify permitted actions; and error defines what counts as good enough. Stating an accuracy rate alone while omitting these conditions may mix different tasks. [T-main; T-full]

Static recovery, dynamical closure, and intervention sufficiency are tested separately. A representation that recovers today’s target need not update to tomorrow; predicting passive evolution does not guarantee that actions chosen under that representation are feasible for every hidden state. These are different questions, not something summarized by “the model is good enough.” [T-main; T-full]

Method 2 requires preservation of material identity, source provenance, inferential premises, and support scope. Real observations, internal teaching examples, simulations, normative claims, and formal proofs do not all become external empirical evidence because the same report contains them. Several charts processing the same upstream observation are not several independent materials either. [T-main; T-full]

At minimum, support scope must be traceable to object, window, and corresponding proposition. Summaries or secondary texts may be the material actually read, but must not stand in for verification of the complete source. Inferential premises should also be listed explicitly to prevent a conclusion from circulating back as its own evidence. [T-main; T-full]

Method 3 requires facts, value judgments, and action eligibility to hold separately. An action’s possible effectiveness does not establish that it is worth doing; a worthwhile goal does not establish effective means or an actor’s authority to execute them. Conversely, lacking execution authority does not prohibit grounded normative recommendations, but those recommendations must not be treated as execution authorization. [T-main; T-full]

These three are responsibilities for model use, not three empirical laws of the world. Whether following them reduces errors or adds unnecessary burdens requires separate evaluation, especially with a specified task and comparison baseline. Tool-completion rates cannot replace outcome testing. [T-main]

Teaching should record “checks complete,” “formal derivation correct,” “real-world premises satisfied,” and “actual net benefit established” separately. This section introduces methods and explicitly artificial examples only; it reports no effectiveness of this framework in any real institution. [T-main]

C.6F1 Task Recovery and Worst-Case Error ​

In briefIf states in a representation fiber require different answers, the representation alone cannot recover the task exactly. With error permitted, the answer depends on output space, loss, and distribution.

Let X be a nonempty set of source states, π:X→Y a deterministic representation with Y=π(X), and q:X→V the target. For each y∈Y, the preimage X_y={x:π(x)=y} is called a representation fiber. The decoder may use only y. It must not read the source state, identity labels, or undeclared history behind the scenes.

Task recovery proposition. There exists g:Y→V such that q=g∘π if and only if q is constant on each fiber. If it exists, g is unique on the actual image Y.

Proof. If q=g∘π, the same representation is passed to the same function, so it gives the same target value. Conversely, on each nonempty fiber define its common target value to be g(y). This definition is unambiguous and gives g(π(x))=q(x) for every x. Each y has a source state, so any g satisfying the condition must take that common value. This proposition establishes only the existence of a function between sets. Whether it can be computed on an infinite domain by an algorithm that always halts, and whether a finite budget suffices, are separate questions.

Finite scalar recovery proposition. Further suppose X is finite, q is real-valued, the decoder can output any real number, and loss is the worst-case absolute error over the entire domain. Let a_y=min_{x∈X_y}q(x) and b_y=max_{x∈X_y}q(x). Then

inf_g max_{x∈X}|q(x)−g(π(x))| = (1/2)max_{y∈Y}(b_y−a_y).

Proof. For any output c, the sum of its distances to the two endpoints is at least b_y−a_y. At least one distance is therefore at least half the range. With c=(a_y+b_y)/2, all target values in the fiber lie between the endpoints, and the maximum distance is exactly half the range. Taking the midpoint in each fiber and then the maximum over all fibers attains the lower bound.

For a nonnegative tolerance ε, a decoder with error at most ε over the entire domain exists if and only if the range of every fiber is at most 2ε. With an infinite state domain, use suprema and check boundedness. Vector values, restricted outputs, average loss, and probability weighting each give a different optimization problem. Half the range cannot serve as a universal score of information loss across domains.

Suppose the target is binary, the loss is zero-one loss, and the finite set of source states is uniformly distributed. Let n_y0 and n_y1 be the counts for the two labels in each fiber. The minimum error rate is |X|⁻¹∑_y min(n_y0,n_y1). Only one label can be chosen for each fiber. Choosing the more frequent label attains the smaller of the two error counts. The error under randomized labeling is a convex combination of these two counts and cannot be lower. This expression describes the given distribution. It does not estimate error frequencies in a real population.

If states in a representation fiber require different answers, the representation alone cannot recover the task exactly. With error permitted, the answer depends on output space, loss, and distribution.

First list assumptions: X is nonempty, π:X→Y is deterministic with Y=π(X), and the target is q:X→V. The fiber X_y={x:π(x)=y} contains all source states merged by the representation. Task-recovery proposition: a g:Y→V satisfying q=g∘π exists if and only if q is constant on every fiber. Necessity: if π(x)=π(x′)=y, then q(x)=g(y)=q(x′). Sufficiency: for each y, define g(y) as the common q value of its fiber. Constancy ensures no ambiguity, and substituting any x gives q(x)=g(π(x)). [T-main]

Uniqueness holds only on the actual image Y: every y comes from at least one x, so q(x) fixes every feasible g. If the target representation space is artificially extended to points with no source state, the theorem does not constrain those points. Existence as a set function also does not guarantee always-halting computability on infinite domains or sufficient finite budgets. [T-main]

The finite scalar worst-case-error proposition additionally requires finite X, real-valued q, any real number as a permitted output, and loss defined as worst absolute error over the full domain. Let a_y and b_y be each fiber's minimum and maximum. The optimal risk is (1/2)max_y(b_y−a_y), not a universal formula for all losses. [T-main]

Lower-bound proof: for any output c, |a_y−c|+|b_y−c|≥b_y−a_y, so at least one endpoint has error ≥(b_y−a_y)/2. Every decoder's full-domain risk is at least the largest half-range. Attainability proof: choose c=(a_y+b_y)/2 on each fiber. All target values lie between the endpoints, each error is ≤the half-range, and equality holds at the endpoints. Thus the lower bound is attained, and the infimum is actually achieved. [T-main]

Given ε≥0, a decoder with full-domain error ≤ε exists if and only if every fiber's range is ≤2ε. Infinite domains require suprema and boundedness checks; vectors, restricted outputs, average loss, and probability weights are different optimization problems. The midpoint is one optimal choice; “the exact-recovery g is unique” must not be transferred into “a globally optimal worst-error g must be unique.” [T-main]

The binary version separately assumes uniformly distributed finite source states and zero-one loss, with each fiber's two class counts n_y0/n_y1. Output 0 makes n_y1 errors, and output 1 makes n_y0 errors; majority choice yields min(n_y0,n_y1). Total risk is Σ_y min(n_y0,n_y1)/|X|. Randomized labels produce only a convex combination of the two error counts and cannot do better; ties permit multiple optimal rules. [T-main]

Uniformity applies to source states, not fibers or representation values. Fibers of different sizes have different weights. Even exact arithmetic describes only this given constructed distribution, not error frequencies in an actual population. Without a reasonable mapping and sample, neither half-ranges nor error rates should be attached to real people. [T-main]

C.7F4 Composition of Conversions and Actual Execution ​

In briefEach step completing its own local task does not guarantee preservation of a common endpoint; representational insufficiency and an incorrect actual rule can also coexist.

The condition concerns only the reachable image f(X). It imposes no constraint on intermediate values without a corresponding source state.

The stages individually complete the tasks of storing and counting. Their composition merges (0,1) and (1,0), so it cannot answer the final query. Retaining the Boolean value at the first position repairs this problem without recovering all source details. Changing the final query to counting is simply a different task that can succeed.

Changing the rule to (0,1) repairs that error without removing the conflict in representation between the first two states. C.8F2 Closure and F5 Error Propagation ​

In briefExact closure requires consistent projected successors for every merged state; approximate closure additionally requires stepwise errors, amplification constants, and valid regions to remain applicable.

Let F:X→X be deterministic one-step dynamics. There exists F̄:Y→Y satisfying π∘F=F̄∘π if and only if π(x)=π(x′) implies π(F(x))=π(F(x′)). The proof follows by replacing the target q in C.6 with π∘F. If the condition holds on the whole domain, then π∘F^t=F̄^t∘π for each nonnegative integer t. The zero-step case is immediate. To go from step t to step t+1, apply the commutation relation once.

If U is the set of permitted actions and each action has a specified F_u, verify the condition for each permitted action before composing the updates step by step for any action sequence fixed in advance. If action feasibility depends on states that were collapsed, also ensure that a macroscopic action can be executed in all relevant source states in the same fiber, or explicitly limit its availability. If different microscopic implementations are grouped under one macroscopic action, check separately whether they give the same macroscopic result.

In a finite stochastic system, let P(x,x′) be the transition kernel. If the probability sum ∑_{x′∈B}P(x,x′) for reaching each target block B is the same for every source state in a given block, define the macroscopic transition kernel using that common value. Whatever the conditional distribution of source states within the block given the current macroscopic block and past records, averaging that common value leaves it unchanged. The projected process therefore updates under this macroscopic kernel. If the same macroscopic one-step law is required for all initial distributions, the blockwise equality condition is also necessary. Check it by starting with all mass at each source state in the same block in turn. Retaining only the next-step mean, or obtaining equality by chance for one initial mixture, does not meet this distributional requirement for all initial states.

Approximate closure also needs a horizon. Let d be a metric on Y. Suppose each reachable x satisfies d(π(F(x)),F̄(π(x)))≤ε, and F̄ satisfies d(F̄(y),F̄(y′))≤L d(y,y′) in the region reached. Starting from y_0=π(x_0), let e_t=d(π(x_t),y_t). The triangle inequality gives e_{t+1}≤ε+Le_t, and the recurrence yields e_t≤ε∑_{j=0}^{t−1}L^j. If the per-step error or L changes over time, adapt the recurrence. The fixed-parameter formula must not still be applied.

These propositions state exact closure and error propagation under the conditions for approximate closure, respectively. They cannot determine whether an empirical model meets the statewise bound, whether it leaves the working region, or whether an intervention belongs to the original operation set. Closure under the usual distribution, closure under intervention, and normative usability still have to be established separately.

C.8 F2 Closure and F5 error propagation

Exact closure requires consistent projected successors for every merged state; approximate closure additionally requires stepwise errors, amplification constants, and valid regions to remain applicable.

Let deterministic one-step dynamics be F:X→X, and let representation π:X→Y use its actual image. There exists F̄ such that π∘F=F̄∘π if and only if x/x′ with the same π still have the same π after updating. Necessity follows because a macroscopic function must give the same output for the same input. For sufficiency, define F̄(y) on each fiber as the common π(F(x)). This applies C.6 with target q replaced by π∘F. [T-main；T-fibre]

If the condition holds throughout the domain, then π∘F^t=F̄^t∘π for every nonnegative integer t. At step zero both sides equal π. Assuming the statement at step t, π∘F^{t+1}=F̄∘π∘F^t=F̄^{t+1}∘π, completing the induction. Holding only in observed samples does not automatically establish validity throughout the domain for arbitrary steps. [T-main]

With allowed actions U, closure must be checked for every explicit F_u before stepwise composition for any prespecified action sequence. Also check whether source states in the same fiber can all implement the macroscopic action, or restrict the available scope. If the same macroscopic action corresponds to different microscopic implementations, consistency of their macroscopic outcomes also requires checking. The same action name does not imply the same implementation map. [T-main]

A finite stochastic system uses a transition kernel P(x,x′). If, for any x/x′ in the same source block, the sums of transition probabilities into each target block B are equal, define the macroscopic kernel by that common value. Sufficiency: even if conditioning on the macroscopic present and past produces different within-block distributions, a weighted average of a constant probability for the same target block remains that constant. The macroscopic one-step update therefore does not depend on the hidden mixture. [T-main]

If the same macroscopic one-step law is required for all initial distributions, equality block by block is also necessary. Start separately from point masses at x and x′ in the same block: the macroscopic present is identical and must have the same distribution over the next block, so the probability sums for every target block must be equal. Preserving only the next-step mean or coinciding by chance under one initial mixture does not satisfy this all-initial-distributions requirement. [T-main]

For the approximate case, let the target space have metric d. Suppose the one-step projection defect d(π(F(x)),F̄(π(x)))≤ε holds for all reached x, and F̄ satisfies a Lipschitz bound L in the region containing the relevant comparison points. Starting from the same initial value y_0=π(x_0), let e_t be the difference between the actual projection and the iterated macroscopic state. The triangle inequality splits the next error into the one-step defect ε and the old deviation amplified macroscopically by Le_t, giving e_{t+1}≤ε+Le_t. [T-main]

Induction from e_0=0 gives e_1≤ε, e_2≤ε(1+L), and e_t≤εΣ_{j=0}^{t−1}L^j. Here L≥0; when L≠1 the sum is (L^t−1)/(L−1), and when L=1 it is t. For L<1 there is a uniform bound ε/(1−L), but this still presupposes continued validity of the domain and stepwise conditions. A large upper bound does not directly prove actual divergence. [T-main；T-bound]

If ε and L vary over time, use the recurrence e_{t+1}≤ε_t+L_t e_t rather than continuing to apply the fixed-parameter formula. Average fitting error is not a per-state upper bound. If a trajectory leaves the working region or a new intervention is outside U, the original guarantee stops where its conditions fail. [T-main]

Exactness, approximation, empirical applicability, and normative usability remain separate. Stochastic closure under ordinary distributions need not cover interventions; a common mathematical update does not establish that an action is feasible or authorized. The kernels, numerical values, and constants below are teaching constructions, not real domain measurements. [T-main]

C.9Different Tasks for Two Queues ​

In briefThe same mean representation can answer total-quantity questions exactly while remaining insufficient for local overload. Adding the maximum repairs some tasks, but does not guarantee closure under location-specific interventions or higher-dimensional dynamics.

Consider two ordered queues (q₁,q₂)∈{0,…,30}², giving 961 states in total. The local overload target is max(q₁,q₂)>15. For the sole purpose of computing expected error, make the 961 source states equiprobable. This differs from making the means equiprobable and does not imply that real queues follow a uniform distribution.

If only the mean m=(q₁+q₂)/2 is retained, (0,20) and (10,10) both give m=10, but the overload answers differ. For a sum s from 16 to 30, the same fiber has 2(s−15) overloaded states and 31−s non-overloaded states. The labels of the other fibers are unambiguous. The minimum number of classification errors is therefore ∑_{s=16}^{20}2(s−15)+∑_{s=21}^{30}(31−s)=85, and the error rate is 85/961≈8.844953%. This is the exact count on the complete artificial state domain.

The rule “report local overload only if the mean exceeds 15” misclassifies 240 states. The 155 errors above the optimal risk for the representation are due to the choice of rule. Those excess errors cannot all be attributed to information loss inherent in aggregation. If the task changes to recovering the maximum queue length, (0,30) and (15,15), both with mean 15, require answers of 30 and 15 respectively. The optimal real-valued output of 22.5 gives a worst-case error of 7.5. If the output must be an integer, the optimal radius for this fiber becomes 8. Output constraints themselves can therefore change the answer.

Adding the maximum M makes it possible to answer local overload and maximum length exactly, but still does not preserve which position is larger. With exactly two queues, the smaller value is 2m−M. If each position serves at most c tasks per round and no new tasks arrive, then

M′=(M−c)_+，m′=[(M−c)_++(2m−M−c)_+]/2,

where a_+=max(a,0). Thus (m,M) has dynamic closure under this symmetric model. When capacity is tied to position as (15,10), the states (0,20) and (20,0) with the same representation become (0,10) and (5,0) respectively. The next representations differ, so closure fails. With three queues, (20,20,5) and (20,15,10) both have mean 15 and maximum 20, but their totals after serving up to 15 queued tasks at each position are 10 and 5 respectively. The two-queue result cannot be extrapolated directly.

This example retains both positive and negative results. A total can answer a query about the total exactly, and adding a task-relevant variable can repair some shortcomings. The same addition need not work for interventions tied to position, heterogeneous dynamics, or more dimensions. Recovery of all microscopic information need not be a success condition for every task.

C.10The Strict Stability Condition for Feedback With Fixed Delay ​

In briefFeedback having an effect does not mean stability. The strict threshold in this fixed-delay linear model excludes equality and does not prove real-world safety or repair.

Consider x_{t+1}=x_t−k x_{t−d}, where d is a fixed nonnegative integer, k is a fixed real number, and the initial history x_{−d},…,x_0 has finite values. The variable x is a signed deviation. The equation has no noise, external input, saturation, or state constraints. The zero solution is asymptotically stable for all initial histories if and only if

0<k<k_c(d)=2sin[π/(4d+2)].

Expanding the state to (x_t,x_{t−1},…,x_{t−d}) yields a finite-dimensional linear recurrence with characteristic polynomial p(λ,k)=λ^{d+1}−λ^d+k. All characteristic roots lie strictly inside the unit circle if and only if the solution tends to zero for every initial history. The modes associated with repeated roots decay despite their polynomial factors when the root modulus is strictly less than 1.

At k=0 there is a root at 1, so the system is not asymptotically stable. For k<0, p(1,k)<0, while p(λ,k) is positive for sufficiently large positive λ. There is therefore a real root greater than 1. For sufficiently small positive k, the root at 1 enters the unit circle as λ=1−k+O(k²). The remaining roots, originally at 0, stay inside the circle. Small positive gains are therefore stable. When d=0, the only root 1−k directly gives 0<k<2.

For positive k, write a boundary root as λ=e^{iθ}, with 0<θ<2π. From k=λ^d(1−λ) we obtain

k=2sin(θ/2)exp{i[(d+1/2)θ−π/2]}.

For k to be positive and real, θ=(π+4πm)/(2d+1) with m=0,…,d. The first boundary at positive gain comes from the smallest angle and its conjugate angle, giving k_c(d). The roots vary continuously with the parameter and are bounded at finite parameter values. They must pass through the boundary before leaving the unit circle.

We must also rule out renewed stability at higher gains. On the unit circle, p_λ=λ^{d−1}[(d+1)λ−d] is nonzero, so boundary roots are simple. Implicit differentiation gives

d log|λ|/dk = (2d+1)(1−cosθ)/{k|(d+1)e^{iθ}−d|²}>0.

Thus all crossings at positive k go outward, with no re-entry. Together with stability at small gains, this gives the necessary and sufficient interval stated above. Equality at the boundary leaves a nondecaying mode. A root modulus slightly below 1 in floating-point arithmetic is no basis for including the boundary. Above the boundary a growing mode exists, while the special all-zero history still remains zero.

With k=0.8 fixed, the critical values for d=0 and 1 are 2 and 1, respectively, and the system is stable. The critical values for d=2 and 3 are approximately 0.618034 and 0.445042, respectively, and the system is unstable. For an all-ones initial history, the first step in all four cases gives 0.2. The feedback term already affects the state in the model, while convergence differs. Even when the system is stable, the result still places no bound on transient peaks or costs and does not establish acceptability to people or real-world repair. Time-varying delays, stochastic disturbances, nonlinearity, and constraints on execution require new analysis.

Feedback having an effect does not mean stability. The strict threshold in this fixed-delay linear model excludes equality and does not prove real-world safety or repair.

The model is x_{t+1}=x_t−k x_{t−d}, where d is a fixed nonnegative integer, k a fixed real number, and the initial history x_{−d},…,x_0 finite; x is a signed deviation. There is no noise, external input, saturation, or state constraint. The theorem states that the zero solution is asymptotically stable for all initial histories if and only if 0<k<k_c(d)=2sin[π/(4d+2)]. [T-main]

Augment the state to (x_t,x_{t−1},…,x_{t−d}), with characteristic polynomial p(λ,k)=λ^{d+1}−λ^d+k. All roots being strictly inside the unit circle is equivalent to convergence to zero for all initial histories: each mode of the linear recurrence is a power of a root, multiplied by a finite-degree polynomial for repeated roots. If the root modulus is <1, the polynomial times the exponential still decays. A modulus ≥1 provides a nondecaying or growing mode, violating the requirement for all histories. [T-main]

First exclude k≤0: at k=0, root 1 retains a constant mode; at k<0, p(1,k)=k<0, while p>0 for sufficiently large positive λ, so continuity gives a real root >1. For d=0, the sole root is 1−k, and |1−k|<1 directly gives 0<k<2. [T-main]

For d≥1, sufficiently small positive k is stable: the simple root 1 at k=0 moves inside the circle by the implicit-function expansion λ=1−k+O(k²); the other d roots vary continuously from 0 and remain inside for sufficiently small k. Continuity here controls the root set; it does not require each zero root to have the same differentiable expansion. [T-main]

To find boundaries at positive k, set λ=e^{iθ}, 0<θ<2π. From p=0, k=λ^d(1−λ). Since 1−e^{iθ}=2sin(θ/2)exp[i(θ/2−π/2)] and sin(θ/2)>0 on this interval, k=2sin(θ/2)exp{i[(d+1/2)θ−π/2]}. Positive real k requires phase to be an integer multiple of 2π, so θ=(π+4πm)/(2d+1), m=0,…,d. [T-main]

The smallest positive-gain boundary comes from the smallest angle π/(2d+1) and its conjugate angle, giving k_c=2sin[π/(4d+2)]. Roots vary continuously with the parameter and remain bounded at finite parameter values, so leaving the circle requires crossing its boundary. Stability therefore holds from small positive k up to the first boundary. [T-main]

Finding only the first exit is insufficient to prove necessity; restabilization must also be excluded. On the boundary, p_λ=λ^{d−1}[(d+1)λ−d] is nonzero: λ≠0, while (d+1)λ=d would require |λ|=d/(d+1)<1. Thus unit-circle boundary roots are simple and can be differentiated implicitly with respect to k. [T-main]

Using λ′=−1/p_λ and k=λ^d(1−λ), obtain (λ′/λ)=(λ−1)/{k[(d+1)λ−d]}. Take the real part and multiply by the conjugate denominator; the numerator’s real part is (2d+1)(1−cosθ). Hence d log|λ|/dk=(2d+1)(1−cosθ)/{k|(d+1)e^{iθ}−d|²}>0. Every unit-circle crossing at positive k is outward, so the number of exterior roots cannot decrease through inward crossings. There is therefore no region of restored stability at larger gain. [T-main]

At exact equality, a unit-modulus nondecaying mode exists. A floating-point root computed as 0.999999… must not be counted as stability. For k>k_c, a growing mode exists, but an all-zero history remains zero. “Not asymptotically stable for all histories” does not mean every special trajectory must diverge. [T-main]

For the source’s k=0.8 example, the critical values for d=0/1 are 2/1, so both are stable; for d=2/3 they are approximately 0.618033989/0.445041868, so both are unstable. C.12Three Limits on Composing Local Successes ​

In briefFunctional composition, causal chaining, and joint resource feasibility are three distinct checks. Knowing that every state has some solution also does not guarantee that current information permits selecting a jointly qualified action.
Codes used in this section (1)
  • F3Common Feasible Actions under Information Constraints

Whether conversions can compose to give the same final answer, whether causal effects can link into a chain, and whether actions can be jointly executed with available resources concern functions, joint responses, and sets of constraints, respectively. They can occur in the same workflow. A generic status of “connected” cannot make them interchangeable.

See §2.4.2 for the causal counterexample. The types s∈{0,1} have equal weight, with m(a)=a(1−s) and y(a,m)=sm. The average effect from treatment to mediator and the average effect from mediator to outcome are both 1/2, whereas the effect of the full chain is 0 because different types carry the two effects. This rules out multiplying edge means unconditionally. It does not withdraw an independently identified total effect.

The resource counterexample consists of two unit tasks, each taking a full period. Both must be completed before the end of the first period, which has capacity of only 1. Each task is feasible alone, but there is no feasible joint schedule. Joint execution becomes feasible if capacity in that period rises to 2, or if one deadline is legitimately moved to the second period and that period has capacity of 1. If both original deadlines remain, adding capacity in the second period cannot make up for the original deadline. The conclusion follows directly from the stated constraints. It does not certify real-world demand, capacity, or authorization.

F3 Common feasible action proposition. For a finite, single-round deterministic task, let X_y be a nonempty observation class and A_ok(x) the set of eligible actions for each state. There exists an action depending only on y that is eligible for every x∈X_y if and only if ∩_{x∈X_y}A_ok(x) is nonempty.

Proof. If such an action a exists, it belongs to the eligible set of every compatible state and therefore to the common intersection. Conversely, choose any a in the nonempty intersection. It is eligible in every compatible state, and choosing it does not require knowing which state actually holds. Nonemptiness of each state’s set separately is only a necessary condition. For example, if the eligible sets of two states are {左} and {右}, meaning left and right, respectively, each has an option but they share no action. If both allow an authorized preservation action, the intersection can be nonempty while the state remains incompletely identified.

The eligible sets in the proposition must already incorporate the resources, protections, and valid permissions required by the task. Writing an action in mathematical terms cannot substitute for these real-world conditions. The proposition concerns only the listed compatible states and cannot guarantee that unknown states will never occur. When undertaken lawfully, further observation, waiting, or preservation can change the information and policy sets. None of these occurs automatically. Probability-based choices that tolerate risk, randomization, and sequential policies are analyzed separately according to their own objectives. Representational sufficiency, computability, accessibility, implementability, resources, and authority to adopt must be justified separately for the specific use.

Understanding this section

C.12 Three Boundaries on Combining Local Successes

Functional composition, causal chaining, and joint resource feasibility are three distinct checks. Knowing that every state has some solution also does not guarantee that current information permits selecting a jointly qualified action.

This section first separates three kinds of “combination”: whether a transformation preserves the same final answer is a functional condition; whether causal effects can chain through mediators is a joint-response condition; and whether actions can be implemented together within resources is a constraint-set condition. One process may need all three, and a generic “connected” state cannot establish them all. [T-main]

The functional boundary returns to C.7: when q=g∘f, the composition h∘f preserves q only if g is constant on the h-fibers in the reachable intermediate domain. Two steps that separately preserve and count do not guarantee retention of a task about the first position. This is a different failure from the causal or resource obstacles below. [T-main; T-compose]

The source’s causal construction takes equally weighted s=0/1, with m(a)=a(1−s) and y(a,m)=s m. The effect of changing treatment a from 0 to 1 on the mediator is 1−s, respectively 1/0 for the two types, averaging 1/2. At fixed a, the outcome effect of changing mediator m from 0 to 1 is s, respectively 0/1, also averaging 1/2. But the whole-chain effect y(1,m(1))−y(0,m(0))=s(1−s)=0, with mean 0 as well. The product of edge means, 1/4, cannot replace the joint response of 0. [T-main; T-causal]

Different types carrying the two segments explains why unconditional multiplication fails. It does not imply that all chain effects are always zero, or revoke an independently identified total effect. If a real task has directly identified its total effect, retain it according to its grounds rather than requiring recertification through a product of edges. [T-main]

The resource construction has two unit tasks, each exclusively occupying one complete time slot, both due by the end of period one, with capacity 1 in that period. Each can be scheduled alone, but total demand 2>1 makes a joint schedule infeasible. Capacity 2 in the same period permits parallel execution; alternatively, lawfully moving one deadline to period two with capacity 1 there permits sequential scheduling. If both original deadlines remain unchanged, adding period-two capacity cannot fill the original deadline gap. [T-main]

F3 is limited to finite, single-round deterministic tasks. Current observation y is compatible with a nonempty state set X_y, and every state has a qualified-action set A_ok(x). There exists one action selected solely from y that is qualified for every compatible state if and only if the intersection ∩_{x∈X_y}A_ok(x) is nonempty. This guarantees the same action across all compatible states, not secretly selecting different actions according to the true hidden state. [T-main]

Necessity: if a common action a exists, it is qualified for every compatible x, hence belongs to every set and their intersection. Sufficiency: select an a from the nonempty intersection; it is qualified for every compatible x without needing to know which x is real. Each A_ok(x) being nonempty is only necessary: the sets {left}/{right} still have an empty intersection. [T-main]

If both states permit an already authorized preservation action, their intersection can be nonempty while the state remains incompletely identified. Thus full state identification is not a universal prerequisite for every bounded action. However, “preservation” cannot be treated as inherently available: its protections, resources, and authority must first actually be checked into A_ok. [T-main]

Qualified sets must already include task goals, protections, resources, and valid authority. Writing an action mathematically does not establish real eligibility. The conclusion covers only listed compatible states, not a guarantee that unknown states can never appear. Lawful additional observation, waiting, or preservation can change the set of information strategies, but neither happens automatically nor is authorized by this proposition itself. [T-main]

Probabilistic choice under risk tolerance, randomization, and sequential strategies involve different goals and problems. Original F3 asks only for one deterministic action qualified in every state; acceptable average risk cannot quietly replace the original all-state guarantee. Representational sufficiency, computability, accessibility, implementability, resources, and authority to adopt must each be argued for the particular use. [T-main]

How the argument develops

  1. 1

    Identify whether the current composition problem concerns functions, causation, or resources, and list their contracts separately.

  2. 2

    For causation, check same-case joint responses; do not multiply edge means unconditionally.

  3. 3

    For resources, check joint scheduling under the same deadlines; late capacity cannot satisfy an earlier deadline.

  4. 4

    List the current nonempty compatible-state set and verified qualified-action sets.

  5. 5

    Take their all-state common intersection and prove both directions; if empty, do not invent a common action.

  6. 6

    Expanded observation or strategies require independently lawful grounds and rechecked goals; retain boundaries concerning unknown states.

If both original tasks are due in period one, late capacity cannot meet the original constraint.

No. Probability and risk objectives need a separate contract; the original proposition guarantees one action across all compatible states.
