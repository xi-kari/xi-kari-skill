# Part 3 Universal Structural Grammar {#part-title-3}

This Part provides a shared descriptive language for discussing the broadly conceived empirical structural world. It does not assert that “all things are isomorphic”; it provides a set of concepts that can each be stated, tested, withdrawn, and converted across scales. Four definitions specify how to state objects and states clearly: structural object, basic state terms, effective feedback, and scale transformation. The eleven basic structural concepts specify which structural positions need to be distinguished in explanation. Definitions and basic concepts, once written down, do not thereby become empirical facts. The eleven concepts are not claimed to be a logically independent, unique, or complete classification of the world; a task may use only as many of them as suffice, or add variables from its own domain after stating the reasons. Empirical support still comes from specific materials, specific tests, and the corresponding evidence procedures.

## 3.1 Structural Object (D0) {#section-3-1}

A structural object is the candidate object that an analysis intends to discuss. Stating it clearly requires seven elements, written as:

S*=&lt;B,X,R,I,T,SP,K&gt;

The boundary (B) specifies which states and relations are counted within the current object and which remain outside it.

The state variables (X) are the variables this analysis has selected and stated; they do not stand for all the actual states of the object.

Relations or transition constraints (R) must state endpoints, direction, time, and conditions of application.

The interface (I) is where the object has inputs, outputs, filtering, transmission, or conversion with its exterior.

The time window (T) is the time range used for observation, prediction, intervention, or feedback.

The scale profile (SP) records the object's positions on the nine scale axes: aggregation, space, time, organization, causation, observation resolution, influence, network topology, and jurisdiction and authorization.

The identity criterion (K) specifies after which changes the object is still counted as the same object.

These seven elements only define the candidate object of this analysis. The definition makes the object available for discussion; it neither provides grounds for object facts nor proves that grouping in this way brings a gain. Naming, clustering, drawing a boundary, and narrative coherence cannot replace the evidence of a grouping-gain test (§4.3). The null model, that is, a comparison model without the claimed factor, is not written into the object's seven elements; it is settled only within a specific comparative claim and belongs to the pre-registration of that test.

The same object can serve different tasks. Each task must also separately state the target being asked about, horizon, environment, admissible operations, error, and evidential requirements. The completeness of the seven elements must not be taken to imply that the object already suffices to answer every question.

Existing records, observations, or other reliable materials can support facts concerning specified people, organizations, events, and their relations; when they are used, their sources and identity conditions must be stated. These facts do not disappear merely because a grouping gain has not yet been tested, nor do they automatically establish that the grouping has a predictive gain, forms a unified subject, or has a common will.

## 3.2 Basic State Terms (D1) {#section-3-2}

The six terms change, operation, persistence, evolution, dissolution, and repair are each used according to detectable criteria stated in advance.

Change is a detectable difference in the selected variables between two observation positions. The term implies nothing about its cause, whether it will last, or its direction.

Operation is a state transition in the state variables while the object's boundary, relations, interfaces, scale position, and identity criterion all hold under the current definition.

Persistence is the current identity criterion continuing to hold within a specified time window. It describes only continuation; it does not mean health, justification, progress, or that continuation is warranted.

Evolution is a detectable change over time in the boundary, variable set, relations, interfaces, scale position, or identity conditions in the object's definition, exceeding a range of fluctuation stated in advance. The definition of evolution does not include durable traces, path dependence, historical determination, a selection mechanism, direction, maturity, progress, decline, or a value judgment. Whether a historical conditional increment beyond the current representation exists requires a separate historical-increment test (§4.5); whether variation, differential retention, and reproduction are present requires a separate check under the corresponding conclusion rule and the selection mechanism (§4.13.1, §6.8). A record of evolution can hold while a historical increment or a selection mechanism still does not hold.

Dissolution means that the identity criterion in the original definition no longer holds. Dissolution may mean the object's termination, its conversion, or the need for reclassification; it does not automatically amount to failure.

Repair is the formation of a new state satisfying a target identity criterion (written K*) or a target functional criterion (written F*) stated in advance. Repair can retain old components, or change boundaries, relations, interfaces, and carriers; it does not require a return to the historical state. Whether repair should occur, what its target should be, who decides, and who carries it out must each undergo review under normative premises and operational procedures; the definition of “repair” does not by itself authorize anyone to carry out repair.

These state terms do not define “structure,” nor do they judge whether a candidate grouping brings a gain. The latter is answered by a pre-registered grouping-gain test, conducted relative to a null model of comparable complexity and within the scale, time window, identity criterion, and extrapolation unit that the test states (§4.3); object facts have separate grounds (§3.1). That a state term applies cannot in turn serve as proof of a grouping gain, nor can it replace independent grounds for the object's existence.

## 3.3 Effective Feedback (D2) {#section-3-3}

Only when a prior state or output returns through a traceable channel and produces a detectable difference in subsequent states, transition probabilities, temporal sequence, or constraints, compared with the absence of that return, is it called effective feedback.

Effective feedback has at least four traceable positions: the prior state or output, the return channel, the receiving position, and the subsequent item that is changed. Reception of a signal, generation of a receipt, recording of an expression, or preservation of an archive establishes only that information arrived or left a trace. Only when a detectable difference actually appears afterward, compared with the absence of return or a blocked channel, does it count as effective feedback. This definition does not guarantee that feedback is accurate, timely, or beneficial, still less that it produces learning.

That feedback has had an effect, that the process is stable, and that the target has improved are three matters that must be judged separately. A return that has changed subsequent states can coexist with oscillation, amplification, or deviation from the target. Fixed rules can also produce feedback; retraining parameters is not a necessary condition. A claim that feedback is correcting deviations must separately state the deviation from the target, gain, execution delay, operating range, and destabilization criteria. A claim that feedback brings improvement must also evaluate transient peaks, cost, and distribution. Stability criteria for a particular linear model constrain only that model. They must not be generalized, merely on the strength of the term “negative feedback,” into a claim that real-world processes necessarily stabilize themselves.

## 3.4 Scale Transformation (D3) {#section-3-4}

A cross-scale judgment must state explicitly the transformation from the source scale to the target scale: what is retained, what is changed, what is lost, which type of transformation is used (one of the nine scale transformations, §5.5), how large the error is, and the range within which it applies. It is written as:

M(SP0→SP1)=&lt;保留变量, 改变变量, 丢失变量, 变换方式, 误差, 适用区间&gt;[^tn-fields-03]

Here SP0 and SP1 are the scale profiles of the source scale and the target scale, respectively.

Each judgment of whether a transformation is sufficient must also specify the particular question or class of tasks it addresses, the horizon, the environment, and the admissible operations. Static answers, dynamic progression, responses to intervention, and feasible action are judged separately.

Using the same word at different scales does not constitute a scale transformation. An aggregated object is not simply an enlarged local object, nor can aggregate statistics be assigned back to every local unit. Each transformation must state what the source scale and the target scale are, whether the object's identity criterion is retained, how relations or units are converted, what information is lost, and under what circumstances the error exceeds the range of applicability. Without bridging evidence, only parallel descriptions of the two scales may be given; successful transfer must not be claimed.

At least three questions must be distinguished for the same mapping: whether it suffices to answer the current static question, whether it can advance to the next state on its own, and whether it still suffices to evaluate admissible interventions. There is no automatic progression among the three. A total can answer a question about the total exactly while omitting local overload. Adding the maximum can recover a particular static answer while still omitting the positional identity needed for targeted operations. The success of a mapping must therefore always be specified with its target, horizon, environment, and admissible actions, not merely with a note that “information is retained.”

### 3.4.1 Representational Recasting and Discursive Recasting {#section-3-4-1}

Representational recasting rewrites a sourced state, information, or model expression, through a specified converter, bridging party, or rule, into a new representation directed toward a target scale, circle[^tn-circle-03], task, or vocabulary system. When the content being recast is primarily language, institutional texts, or explanation, it is called discursive recasting. The new representation must retain its parent record (the record one level up from which it directly comes) and its source lineage.

Recasting differs from scale transformation, transformation of circle relations, network propagation, compression, and lateral analogical transfer. These operations may participate in a recasting, but cannot replace an account of the recasting itself. Whether a recasting actually changes understanding or action on the target side still requires support from materials concerning the specified channel. Whether the collapsed differences still carry conditional increments, and whether different orderings give different results, are still answered by the existing scale-closure test (§4.6).

A recasting needs to state at least: the source formulation and its context; the target circle and task; the converter or conversion rule, and which version of the rule is used; what is kept unchanged; what is allowed to change; the collapsed differences; omissions; added explanations; ambiguities; error; the parent record; the path back to the source record; review on the target side; the validity period; and the conditions for failure and withdrawal. Without independent review on the target side, it counts only as a recasting awaiting testing.

</div>

## 3.5 The Eleven Basic Structural Concepts {#section-3-5}

</div>

<SectionLesson section="3.5" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 3.5.1 Candidate Object (U01) {#section-3-5-1}

The function of this concept is to turn an object of intended study into a traceable starting point for analysis: its definition in terms of the seven elements of a structural object must be complete, with a statement of how it is traced across scales. When the support of a formal test is sought, the pre-registration of that test is made separately, and the null model is settled at that point. A candidate object may have soft boundaries, be short-lived, have multiple centers, or be distributed, but such groupings are not presupposed to bring a gain; completing the definition of a candidate object does not guarantee in advance that a grouping gain holds either. This concept is not applicable to purely metaphysical objects for which empirical elements are refused. Once the definition is complete, all that can be done is to record the object as a candidate and propose questions awaiting testing; it does not generate value, responsibility, choice, or authorization to act.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="3.5.1" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 3.5.2 Boundaries and Interfaces (U02) {#section-3-5-2}

Boundaries distinguish the inside of an object from its outside; interfaces record exchanges and constraints across the boundary. Boundaries can be permeable, movable, nested, or overlapping; interfaces can filter, delay, convert, or block processes across the boundary. Spatial proximity does not automatically constitute a shared boundary. The existence of a boundary does not imply that the object is closed; still less does the existence of an interface imply that any one party holds control rights. Across scales, the distinction between inside and outside and the traceability of channels should be retained, while allowing boundaries and interfaces to be reorganized. This concept is not applicable to objects for which no distinction between inside and outside can be stated.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="3.5.2" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 3.5.3 States and Observables (U03) {#section-3-5-3}

States are described through stated variables and observables, with measurement positions and errors retained. A state is the object's configuration, recorded in terms of the selected variables, at a given time window and resolution; an observable is a record formed from a state through a measurement channel. Measurable quantities are not the same as the entire state; not having been observed is not the same as not existing; and a record may also include contributions from the measurement process itself. Changes of scale can alter resolution and observables, but variable definitions, measurement positions, and errors must be retained through the transfer.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="3.5.3" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 3.5.4 Components, Carriers, and Substrates (U04) {#section-3-5-4}

Stating what realizes a structure requires distinguishing three things. A component is a constituent unit of the current object. A carrier is a material, informational, or relational position through which a specified state transition actually passes; it carries the causal transition. A substrate provides the background conditions that make this realization possible. A given component is not necessarily the key carrier; after a component is replaced, the object may still satisfy its identity criterion. Identifying a carrier specifies only where the realization occurs; it must not be taken to mean that the carrier has intent, bears responsibility, or has an obligation to continue carrying. This concept is not applicable to purely definitional objects that make no claim to empirical realization.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="3.5.4" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 3.5.5 Relations and Constraints (U05) {#section-3-5-5}

Relations describe repeatable dependencies between endpoints; constraints narrow the range of feasible states or transitions. A relation counts as a structural constraint only when it changes what states or transitions are feasible. Correlation, adjacency, and co-occurrence cannot by themselves be upgraded to causal constraints; to call a relation causal also requires temporal order and a specified channel, together with intervention or a counterfactual comparison. The existence of a constraint does not establish that it is justified, either. Scale transformation may change effective relations, so the endpoints, direction, and applicable window of a relation must remain traceable.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="3.5.5" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 3.5.6 Flow, Channels, and Transmission (U06) {#section-3-5-6}

In systems where something is claimed to transfer across components or boundaries, flow specifies what resource, signal, or effect is transferred, together with its direction, rate, and distribution; a channel specifies the path it takes; and transmission specifies how inputs change downstream states. Capacity, delay, reliability, controllability, and loss are candidate attributes measured channel by channel and dimension by dimension; unmeasured dimensions remain unknown. This concept provides only descriptive positions, not proof that any particular channel actually exists; whether a channel has an effect must still be answered by a channel-effect test (§4.4). The source, target, and channel need to remain traceable through scale transformation. Nor can a channel automatically be taken to be a responsible party.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="3.5.6" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 3.5.7 Feedback and State Updating (U07) {#section-3-5-7}

Feedback is a return relation: a prior state or output constitutes effective feedback (§3.3) only when it returns through a channel and changes subsequent states, probabilities, or constraints. State updating is the subsequent change caused by a return or other input. The two need to be stated separately. Feedback can be reinforcing, inhibiting, delayed, or distorted, and it can also be blocked.

Effective feedback is not the same as learning, nor is it the same as repair. A claim that it has produced learning has two routes: the carrier route checks current updates, subsequent invocation, and task results under §4.5; the historical-increment route additionally requires updates that can be retained, repeated rounds, and the support of a historical-increment test (see §4.11.3, §6.3). A claim that it has achieved repair additionally requires a target identity criterion or target functional criterion stated in advance, together with review under normative premises and operational procedures. This concept is not applicable to a one-way process with no return channel.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="3.5.7" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 3.5.8 Conditions and Environment (U08) {#section-3-5-8}

A condition is a local prerequisite for a mechanism to hold in the current case. The environment is the set of variables left outside the current boundary that may affect the object through interfaces. The environment and conditions enter explanation only when they change the object's states or transitions through detectable interfaces. The environment is not a personified overall cause, nor does it determine every outcome. When the scale changes, the line between object and environment is redrawn, so the relevant environmental variables and the interfaces through which they act must be stated again. For a model that is explicitly closed and has no external channel, this concept may be marked not applicable.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="3.5.8" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 3.5.9 Load, Capacity, and Recovery Margin (U09) {#section-3-5-9}

In nonequilibrium systems with sustained flux that require maintenance or recovery, load must be described together with finite capacity and recovery margin. Load is demand of the same type at a given position and within a given time window. Capacity is the range of what can be taken up, in the same type or through an explicit conversion relation. Recovery margin is the room still available after a disturbance for replenishment, reorganization, or a return to a state that satisfies the target identity criterion or target functional criterion. Units, distributions, time windows, and bottleneck positions must be stated for all three. An instantaneous shortfall does not automatically amount to cumulative damage, nor does overload create an obligation to continue carrying. An object with neither sustained nonequilibrium nor maintenance requirements may be marked not applicable.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="3.5.9" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 3.5.10 Trace, Memory, and Paths (U10) {#section-3-5-10}

A trace is a durable record that a prior change leaves in a state, relation, boundary, interface, transition constraint, or environment. A trace counts as structural memory only when it enters subsequent transitions. Historical-increment path differences are determined under the historical increment (§4.5) only when history still changes future paths, probabilities, or conversion costs after controlling for the stated current state. General historical effects transmitted through measured current carriers are tested separately under §4.5. That a trace has been preserved is not the same as a historical increment holding; nor is a path difference the same as a fate already sealed or irreparability. The carriers that bear traces and the threshold for judging something “durable” can change with scale. This concept is not applicable to static classifications with no temporal extension.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="3.5.10" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 3.5.11 Phase, Transition, and Dissolution (U11) {#section-3-5-11}

A phase is a conditional range of operation under the same identity criterion, distinguished by variables and criteria stated in advance. A transition is a detectable change between ranges that exceeds the differences that noise and the choice of binning could produce. Dissolution means only that the original identity criterion no longer holds. The basic phase pattern does not require path dependence. A claim that a particular trigger caused a transition separately requires a channel-effect test. Sustained effects or delayed recovery transmitted through current carriers require separate evidence under §4.5; a historical-increment test is additionally required only when a historical increment beyond the current representation is claimed. A phase is not a maturity ranking of the seven state prototypes (§8.3), nor a stage that every object must pass through. If the identity criterion has already ceased to hold before a transition, the case should be recorded instead as object conversion or dissolution.

</div>

## 3.6 Combining Basic Concepts and Purpose-Relative Closure {#section-3-6}

The first three concepts (“Candidate Object”; “Boundaries and Interfaces”; “States and Observables”) answer what is currently treated as a candidate object, and how it is seen. The middle three (“Components, Carriers, and Substrates”; “Relations and Constraints”; “Flow, Channels, and Transmission”) answer what realizes the structure, and through what change is transmitted. The next four (“Feedback and State Updating”; “Conditions and Environment”; “Load, Capacity, and Recovery Margin”; “Trace, Memory, and Paths”) answer how a return updates states, how conditions act, how load is distributed, and how history leaves effects. The last (“Phase, Transition, and Dissolution”) answers when it is still the same range of operation, and when there is transition, conversion, or dissolution.

These concepts connect with one another, but do not prove one another. Whenever one is missing, it must be marked as unknown, not applicable, unobservable, or withheld for protection (§2.9), with an account of how this affects the ceiling on conclusions. The familiarity of everyday language must not be used to fill in what is missing, nor may a stage, value, responsibility, choice, or authorization be inferred directly from the fact that a concept applies.

[^tn-fields-03]: Translator's note: The formula retains its original labels. In order, these are retained variables, changed variables, lost variables, the type of transformation, error, and applicable range.

[^tn-circle-03]: Translator's note: Circles may be parallel, nested, overlapping, or bridged; the term does not imply a hierarchy.
