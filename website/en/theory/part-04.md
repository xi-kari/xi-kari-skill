# Part 4 Empirical Questions, Conclusion Rules, and Conditional Formal Propositions {#part-title-4}

There are four kinds of empirical question: whether a candidate grouping brings a conditional gain, called grouping gain for short; whether a specified channel has an identifiable effect on a target transition, called channel effect for short; whether history provides conditional increments beyond the current representation, called historical increment for short; and under which tasks, dynamics, and interventions a specified representation has closure, called scale closure for short. These four kinds of question are not themselves laws governing all objects. Each formal test states two things together: the factual assertion awaiting testing, and the rules for judging the evidence. The two must not be conflated in writing: whether an effect exists is determined by the world and the conditions at issue; whether the available evidence suffices to support that assertion is determined by the research design and results.

This Part separates empirical testing, the use of results, and formal proof. Formal tests answer factual questions; conclusion rules state which limited conclusions can be drawn when the existing grounds are put together; conditional formal propositions state relations that necessarily hold under explicitly stated mathematical conditions. Recording the same result again does not add independent evidence; nor does the number of empirical questions passed, or of conclusion rules met, measure the usefulness of the entire framework.

## 4.1 Pre-Registration and Outcome Records for Formal Tests {#section-4-1}

This section provides a standard recording procedure for confirmatory testing. Under this procedure, each formal test has two sections. The first is the pre-registration: before the results are seen, the test's target, criteria, and rules are set down. The second is the outcome record: it is appended after the test has been carried out, and what was set down earlier is kept unchanged. What has not yet been done is explicitly marked “not yet evaluated”; times, observed values, or materials must not be fabricated. The two sections together determine what outcomes count as support, what outcomes count as refutation, and where conclusions may be extrapolated.

This procedure takes a single primary criterion as its default design, to prevent choosing favorable results after they appear; it is not the only reasonable form for every study. Multiple outcomes, multiple models, joint criteria, confirmatory work following exploratory work, and sequential studies can each have valid designs, but the primary target or joint rule, the selection procedure, the stopping rule, and the applicable handling of multiple comparisons must be stated in advance. The subtypes of the four empirical questions and of the human empirical hypotheses below are all written with a single primary criterion and belong to this standard procedure. Materials outside the procedure are still evaluated according to their actual research designs; they must not be judged to have no knowledge value merely because their records take a different form. Nor are the four empirical questions claimed to exhaust all scientific questions or to be logically independent axioms.

The pre-registration must fix the following seven aspects before the results are seen.

Identity and finalization of the test. State which of the four empirical questions this test falls under and which specific test it is, so that the specific test is kept distinct from the abstract type of question. Once finalized, the pre-registration is not changed; finalization precedes access to the results, and this can be checked. Only in this way can it be shown that the rules were written before the evidence.

Candidate object. Point to the candidate object tested and its definition (§3.1), preventing a name from being treated as grounds for a grouping gain or for object facts.

Scale and target. Fix the scale profile, the time window, the identity criterion, and the variables to be explained.

Subtype and success criterion. Before evidence appears, select only one subtype, then only one success criterion from those admissible for that subtype; and state the candidate proposition, the null model, the controls, and the model classes used. The null model is a comparison model without the claimed factor.

Sampling and extrapolation. State the sampling unit and the extrapolation unit separately: where the data come from and how far the conclusion may be generalized are two different things.

Decision rules. State the evaluation metric, the positive threshold, the decision rule, and the null-conclusion rule. The decision rule governs how to determine whether the positive criterion has passed; the null-conclusion rule governs what conditions must be met before the null model can be supported; the positive criterion and the null-conclusion criterion are written separately. The primary target or aggregation rule, the rule for model selection or ensembling, the handling of multiple comparisons, the equivalence or sufficiency test, the requirements for statistical power or sensitivity, and the tolerance are all fixed in advance.

Evidence mode and failure conditions. Explain whether this test is exploratory, confirmatory, a replication, or a falsification, and what the ceiling on conclusions is for each; and state what result counts as falsification and under what circumstances the test is suspended.

As the test proceeds, the status of the pre-registration and any deviations from it also need to be stated. The pre-registration has four possible statuses: draft; finalized before evidence; completed as finalized; deviated after evidence. Each deviation is recorded item by item: what deviated, when, why, and with what impact. Where there is no deviation, this too needs to be stated explicitly as “none.”

The outcome record needs to cover two aspects.

Results. The results are obtained after the pre-registration has been finalized. Record only the results observed under the primary target and rules fixed in advance; state separately whether the positive criterion passed and whether the three null-conclusion criteria (equivalence or sufficiency, statistical power or sensitivity, and tolerance) passed; finally, assign the result to one of the four outcome statuses.

Evidence and analysis materials. Point to the external materials relied on, and to the data, code, models, or logs fixed after the cutoff; all of these need to correspond to real material. The framework's internal concept codes, names that only look like a test in format, and self-claimed conclusions cannot replace a specific test.

Under the single-primary-criterion procedure, there is only one selection rule: before results appear, one subtype and one success criterion are selected. A joint or multi-outcome design must state its overall criterion separately; several individual items that did not pass must not be reassembled after the fact into a success. The evaluation metric, the positive threshold, the null model, the decision rule, the null-conclusion rule, and the falsification conditions are all fixed in advance around the selected criterion. If the results are unfavorable, several subtypes or success criteria must not be combined so as to take whichever is favorable, nor may the target, model, metric, or threshold be changed to rescue the kind of empirical question being tested.

The decision rule also closes off the room for choice that multiple targets and models leave open. If there is more than one target variable, a single primary target or an aggregation rule must be fixed before results; if there is more than one model class, a rule for model selection or ensembling must be fixed; if there are multiple comparisons, a correction rule must be fixed. Without these rules, a test can have only one primary target and one primary model. The null-conclusion rule states separately what equivalence or sufficiency test must be passed, what statistical power or sensitivity must be reached, and within what tolerance, before the null model can be supported. After the test has been carried out, the outcome of the positive criterion must be recorded item by item: the threshold, the observed value, whether it passed, and the analysis materials relied on; whether each of the three null-conclusion criteria passed is also recorded separately. Failure to pass the positive criterion is not the same as the null model having obtained support.

The outcome of a formal test is assigned to one of four statuses. Supported: an eligible test has passed the positive criterion. Not supported or unresolved: the positive criterion has not passed, and the null-conclusion criteria were not fixed in advance or have not all passed. Null conclusion supported: equivalence or sufficiency, statistical power or sensitivity, and tolerance have all passed. Not yet evaluated: this means only that the test has not yet been carried out. These four statuses are only summaries and do not replace the specific conclusion: which criteria fixed in advance passed, which were countered by evidence, and which remain unresolved only because of insufficient precision or materials must be stated separately; clear counterevidence and an inability to judge must not be conflated as one kind of evidence. The outcome record must be linked to the evidence and analysis materials relied on, and it must be possible to find out when the results were obtained, which finalized version of the pre-registration they rest on, and what deviations occurred. Neither an abstract type of question nor a correctly formatted test number can produce support on its own.

Whether a test can be used to support a conclusion rule also depends on whether it has support eligibility. Positive support comes only from a confirmatory test or a replication test, and all of the following need to hold together: before the results were seen, the pre-registration was already in the status “finalized before evidence,” and the content and time of finalization can be checked; the status at the end of the test is “completed as finalized”; the positive criterion is recorded as passed; the outcome status has been stated; and the external evidence and analysis materials correspond to real material. A falsification test may support only a pre-registered falsification judgment or null-model judgment; it must not be used in reverse to rescue a positive empirical claim. An exploratory test, a test whose pre-registration is still a “draft,” a test with the status “deviated after evidence,” or a test with undisclosed deviations can only be used to propose candidates, record deviations, or remain unresolved; where a branch of a conclusion rule requires formal-test eligibility, they must not serve as such support. The framework's internal definitions, concept labels, and the framework's descriptions of itself do not constitute empirical support for a test.

The scale invariants shared by the four empirical questions include the subtype selected in advance, the success criterion, the decision rule, and the null-conclusion rule; these must be retained during scale transfer and must not be replaced. An appeal against a test may challenge the candidate object, subtype, success criterion, null model, primary target or aggregation, model selection or ensembling, multiple comparisons, controls, positive threshold, null-conclusion rule, data split, extrapolation unit, which version of the pre-registration the test rests on, the pre-registration status, the deviation record, and the falsification conditions. If any of these is shown to have been changed after the results appeared, support eligibility must be reduced; a version produced after the fact must not overwrite a record already finalized.

## 4.2 Testing Human Empirical Hypotheses {#section-4-2}

Three of the six human-domain clauses (§7.1) are empirical hypotheses: “Meaning and Coordination,” “Power, Mediation, and Reflexivity,” and “Historical Trace Carriers.” They do not belong to the four universal empirical questions, but they are likewise only abstract hypotheses: they cannot become true through names, through filling in the items of the human variables, or through framework definitions. Their tests form a separate kind, which admits only these three clauses and needs to state which of them is being tested. Apart from this, the two sections of pre-registration and outcome record, the marking of missing items (§2.9), no changes after finalization, the pre-registration status, the four outcome statuses, the disclosure of deviations, the positive criterion, the null-conclusion criterion, and the requirements for evidence and analysis materials are all the same as in §4.1. The corresponding tests are called the “Meaning and Coordination” test, the “Power, Mediation, and Reflexivity” test, and the “Historical Trace Carriers” test. Empirical truth belongs to these specific tests, not to the abstract clauses.

These tests must likewise preselect, before the results are seen, only one subtype and one success criterion for that subtype. The following table is the standard classification under the single-primary-criterion procedure, not an exhaustive classification of meaning, power, or historical carriers. If the object or research design at issue falls outside what the table lists, the testable question and the research procedure must be stated separately; it must not be forced into a favorable category.

| Clause | Subtype or Outcome Family of Which Exactly One Must Be Preselected | Admissible Success Criteria | Stopping Point for the Conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning and Coordination | Choose one of three: resource allocation outcomes, action-choice outcomes, or coordination outcomes | Whichever of “effect of the meaning arrangement on resource allocation,” “effect of the meaning arrangement on action choice,” and “effect of the meaning arrangement on coordination outcomes” corresponds to the selected subtype | Determine only a conditional orientation anchor within the specified object, scale, time window, and outcome family; do not infer a unified inner state, genuine consent, or a common will |
| Power, Mediation, and Reflexivity | Choose one of four: evidence coverage, safety of expression, object behavior, or reflexive response | Whichever of “effect of position or mediation on evidence coverage,” “effect of position or mediation on the safety of expression,” “behavioral response to mediation or publicity,” and “reflexive response to observation or publication” corresponds to the selected subtype | Determine only masking, amplification, behavior, or reflexive response within the specified position, mediation, disclosure conditions, channel, and outcome family; do not infer malice, responsibility, or automatic real-world action |
| Historical Trace Carriers | First preselect only one of the five carrier families as the subtype: institutional or textual record; role or organizational arrangement; habit or practice; trauma record; collective memory carrier. Then identify one specific carrier belonging to that family | Choose one of three: “stays above the threshold over the pre-registered window,” “detected again across pre-registered windows,” or “persists after the event or exposure ends” | Determine only candidate traces within the specified carrier, observables, and time window; obtain separate evidence under §4.5 for the carrier's later effects; only a claim of a historical increment beyond the current representation additionally requires a historical-increment test |

A “Meaning and Coordination” test must not treat “any favorable result establishes the claim” as a rule across the three outcomes of resources, action, and coordination. A “Power, Mediation, and Reflexivity” test must not select favorable results after the fact among evidence coverage, safety of expression, behavior, and reflexive response. A “Historical Trace Carriers” test must not search across multiple carriers or durability criteria for a surviving result. The specific carrier it points to must be exactly one: it must not be left empty, nor may it be a list; and the family to which that carrier belongs must be the very subtype selected. If these two conditions are not met, the test is already ineligible before the results are seen. Multiple targets, multiple models, and multiple comparisons still require a single primary target or aggregation rule, a rule for model selection or ensembling, and a correction rule to be fixed in advance in the decision rule. When the positive criterion does not pass, the default is “not supported or unresolved”; only when equivalence or sufficiency, statistical power or sensitivity, and tolerance in the pre-registered null-conclusion rule have all passed is the result “null conclusion supported.” An exploratory test, a test with the status “deviated after evidence,” or a test with undisclosed deviations produces only candidates or remains unresolved; it must not make the corresponding routes of the human variables “Orientation Anchor” (§7.3.3) and “Conditional Potential Field” (§7.3.8), or the route of historical trace carriers, into empirically established conclusions.

The other three human-domain clauses do not enter these tests. “Action Uptake and Classification of Responsibility” is a rule for classifying uptake and responsibility; “Institutional Write-Back” is a classification rule; “Open-Ended Undertaking” is a normative boundary. They may constrain how the human variables are classified and where they stop, but cannot produce support for an empirical mechanism merely because a classification has been made. Even if a test of a human empirical hypothesis has the outcome “supported,” it increases only the strength of description or explanation; it does not generate value, responsibility, obligation, normative justification, authorization, or real-world action.

## 4.3 Conditional Gain from Candidate Grouping (G1) {#section-4-3}

This kind of question is called grouping gain for short. Its proposition is as follows: in a formal test whose object, scale, time window, identity criterion, candidate grouping, and threshold have all been set down in advance, if the candidate grouping still achieves the predetermined out-of-sample gain relative to a null model of comparable complexity after controlling for the common environment, sampling or classification bias, and grouping leakage, and passes boundary-perturbation checks and review using independent time blocks or independent locations, then the grouping receives limited grouping-gain support under that scale, time window, and identity criterion.

A grouping-gain test does not test “whether this object ultimately exists in the world.” It tests whether the current candidate grouping explains predetermined out-of-sample differences better than the null model; the null model here is of comparable complexity and does not use information from the grouping. The test has two subtypes: the predictive-gain subtype permits only “out-of-sample predictive gain” as its success criterion, and the intervention-gain subtype permits only “out-of-sample intervention gain.” Neither subtypes nor criteria may be exchanged after results appear. The common environment, sampling procedure, classification bias, grouping leakage, and model complexity must all enter the controls or competing explanations.

The minimum positive support requires predetermined evaluation metrics and thresholds; an out-of-sample gain relative to the null model; robustness to boundary perturbation; review using independent time blocks or independent locations; and support eligibility for the test (§4.1). If the out-of-sample gain does not exceed the positive threshold, or is not reproduced under boundary perturbation or independent review, the default is only that grouping gain is not supported or that the result is unresolved. The null model may be supported, or this grouping-gain test refuted, only when the equivalence or sufficiency test and the requirements for statistical power, sensitivity, and tolerance in the pre-registered null-conclusion rule have all passed. If any of the object, grouping, success criterion, null model, decision rules, thresholds, data split, or leakage controls was not fixed before results, the test is suspended.

Passing a grouping-gain test allows only three things to be determined: how strong the support for grouping gain is within the specified scale, time window, and identity criterion; how robust that gain is to the boundary perturbations tested; and the grouping's applicable window. Such support is only an eligibility relative to the declared explanatory or foresight task, not an overall threshold for object existence or object facts (see §10.1). It does not permit an ultimate ontological verdict, nor does it generate a subject, target, value, responsibility, or authorization.

## 4.4 Specified-Channel Effects and Per-Dimension Boundedness (G2) {#section-4-4}

This kind of question is called channel effect for short. Its proposition is as follows: in a formal test whose carrier or channel has been specified in advance, the channel is determined to be a candidate for causally carrying that transition only when the preselected controlled perturbation, controlled intervention, or natural variation meeting identification conditions produces a difference in the specified state transition that exceeds a predetermined threshold. Capacity, delay, reliability, controllability, and loss must be measured separately, channel by channel and dimension by dimension; unmeasured dimensions are not established merely because the proposition holds.

A channel-effect test has two subtypes, and it likewise must preselect only one subtype and one success criterion before results appear (§4.1). For the controlled-identification subtype, what is pre-registered is a controlled means of identification, and its success criterion must be exactly one preselected from “controlled-perturbation effect” and “controlled-intervention effect”; “either perturbation or intervention succeeds” must not be written as a criterion that, after the fact, accepts whichever of the two holds. For the natural-variation subtype, what is pre-registered is natural variation meeting identification conditions, and its only admissible success criterion is “channel effect of natural variation that meets identification conditions.” Both subtypes must first specify the channel, target transition, quantity type and unit, comparative threshold, and dimensions to be measured. The null model is written only for the selected criterion: the preselected perturbation, intervention, or natural variation does not produce a difference in the target transition exceeding the threshold. Criteria not preselected do not enter the success or failure of this test. Boundedness in each dimension separately takes “no difference” or a baseline fixed in advance as its null model.

The question of channel effect does not claim that all causal processes have currently visible channels, nor that all channels are bounded in every dimension. Measuring delay alone does not permit capacity, reliability, controllability, and loss to be declared established along with it. Shifting may be traced further only for quantities of the same type, or quantities with explicit type conversion, units, an accounting boundary, and accounting rules. If the preselected criterion does not produce a difference above the threshold, the default is only to record the positive channel effect as “not supported or unresolved”; this test may be refuted only if it was pre-registered for falsification or a null judgment and the corresponding null-conclusion rule has passed. When common inputs, alternative channels, or measurement protocols can explain the difference, support for the candidate channel should be withdrawn; criteria that were not preselected cannot be used to rescue it, even if their results are favorable. A channel-effect test can locate only candidate causal carrying and the limits on measured dimensions; it cannot create a unified cost across domains, still less turn a carrier into a responsible party.

A channel-effect test tests specified channels and measured dimensions; it is not the sole entry point into causal knowledge. If an eligible comparison identifies the total effect of a treatment but cannot yet distinguish which channel it passes through, the total effect should be retained and the channel marked unresolved; mechanisms must not be fabricated to make up a channel-effect test. If the total effect is near zero, that fact alone cannot negate local processes that offset or compensate for one another; these processes still require independent evidence. When the way the intervention is implemented, common inputs, selection, or measurement conditions change, the current claim is reviewed again; switching afterward to a favorable criterion cannot recover the original confirmatory eligibility.

## 4.5 Historical Formation, Current Carriers, and Historical Increments (G3) {#section-4-5}

Research into the effects of history first distinguishes three questions: how earlier experiences formed the current state; how current carriers retain, update, and continue to affect later outcomes; and whether historical materials still provide additional information or effects given the stated current representation. The first two can hold while the third does not; nor can lack of support for the third establish, in reverse, that the first two hold. The question of historical increment tests only the third; it cannot replace all research into historical causation, learning, accumulation, continuation, or reproduction. The full name of this third question is “historical conditional increment beyond the current representation,” where “the current representation” means the current state as stated on this occasion; where the book speaks of a “historical residual” or a “conditional residual,” it means this same item.

A historical-increment test has two subtypes, and it likewise must preselect only one subtype and one success criterion before results appear (§4.1).

For the subtype of predictive path dependence, the proposition is: if historical variables have been defined before future outcomes are observed, and, after controlling for the stated current state, environment, and measurement protocol, still provide reviewable out-of-sample conditional-increment information for a predetermined outcome, they can support predictive path dependence. Its only admissible success criterion is “historical conditional predictive gain.”

Beyond that conditional increment, the subtype of causal path dependence must also preselect one criterion among history erasure, history recovery, and an equivalent intervention; it supports causal path dependence only when that preselected causal criterion changes the outcome and exceeds the predetermined threshold. The corresponding success criteria are “history-erasure effect,” “history-recovery effect,” and “equivalent-intervention effect,” of which only one may be preselected; it must not wait for results and then select a favorable result from erasure, recovery, or an equivalent intervention.

The null model of both subtypes is that the stated current state already constitutes a sufficient statistic and historical terms provide no conditional increment. The null model of causal path dependence adds one more condition: its own preselected intervention criterion does not change the outcome. Criteria not preselected play no part in success or failure.

The preservation of traces is not the same as their entering later transitions; a predictive gain is not the same as history having causal efficacy, either. If historical variables are defined only after future results have been seen, current-state information is plainly omitted, or a subtype and success criterion have not been uniquely preselected, any one of these suspends the test. If historical terms provide no positive increment after controlling for the current state, environment, and measurement protocol, or if the criterion preselected for the subtype of causal path dependence does not pass the positive threshold, the default record is only “not supported or unresolved”; a corresponding refutation is formed only when the falsification rule or null-conclusion rule fixed in advance has been followed to completion. Other unselected criteria cannot be used to rescue the claim. Passing a historical-increment test permits only the determination of predictive or causal path differences within the pre-registered time window; it does not permit inferences of historical fate, absolute irreversibility, responsibility, or authorization.

Scope of application: predictive path dependence and causal path dependence are this framework's narrow classifications for “historical conditional increments beyond the current representation,” not definitions of all historical causation. Suppose earlier experience H changes current skill S, and S then affects outcome Y. If S already carries the relevant historical information sufficiently, H may add no further predictive information about Y given S. The narrow condition for a historical increment is then not satisfied, but the historical formation process H→S→Y and learning in its general sense may still be supported by independent process materials or a research design. The total historical effect, current carrier, and conditional residual should be reported separately; a historical increment must not be manufactured by omitting known S. 〔Studies 03.03, 04.01–04.04, 24.04, 25.08〕

Intertemporal judgments follow two routes, each stated separately: the carrier-transmission route and the historical-increment route. The carrier-transmission route requires four kinds of material: a measured current state or carrier; grounds for how earlier inputs or changes entered that carrier; the carrier's retention or updating within the declared time window; and longitudinal materials showing its continued effects on predetermined outcomes. Seeing only a lasting outcome, or only the existence of a carrier, cannot confirm the transmission mechanism. A causal claim must additionally compare interruption, replacement, recovery, or natural variation meeting identification conditions under the conditions for causal claims (§2.4), and address common inputs, sustained exposure, selection, attrition, and measurement changes. When there are only correlational materials without the corresponding basis for identification, the conclusion stops at an intertemporal description or a candidate mechanism.

Applying for formal mechanism support for the carrier routes of the conclusion rules below additionally requires the relevant channel and outcome to be supported by an eligible channel-effect test, or by the test of a human empirical hypothesis (§4.2) that the relevant human variable specifies. A “Historical Trace Carriers” test supports only traces within the selected carrier and time window; on its own it cannot satisfy the requirements for subsequent effects or causation. The original test's object, outcome, and time window must cover the claim now being made: a single immediate effect must not be extrapolated to a long-term effect, nor may several separately established local links be treated as a connected chain as a whole. Existing domain evidence still retains its own conclusions and limits under §2.1.1; the separation into routes in this section does not automatically confer on it eligibility as a formal test or as a test of a human empirical hypothesis.

The historical-increment route separately tests, under the requirements of a historical-increment test, conditional increments beyond the current representation, with predictive and causal conclusions still kept separate. Carrier transmission can hold while historical increment is not supported; failure of the positive criterion for historical increment does not establish that carrier transmission holds, either. Known current states must not be omitted to obtain a historical increment, and routes must not be switched after results to rescue the original claim. Each route separately states its grounds, competing explanations, failure conditions, and ceiling on conclusions. Accumulation, continuation, learning, delayed recovery, and intertemporal reproduction cannot acquire support merely by being named.

Historical increments depend on how the current representation is chosen; they must not be treated as an essence of the system independent of representation. Let the complete current state be `x_t`, the stated representation be `z_t=π(x_t)`, and the retained history be `h_t`. If history still provides an increment for a future target given `z_t`, this may be because `h_t` recovers current differences collapsed by `π`; that in itself does not establish that the complete dynamics contains an additional history term. Proportionate comparisons should be made among available, task-relevant current carriers, measurement errors, and model complexity. Known current-state information must not be deliberately deleted to manufacture historical gains.

In reality, the complete state is usually unavailable. Thus, “can in principle be expanded into a sufficient state” must not be treated as having already established the absence of historical increments in this case. If the materials support only an increment relative to the stated current state, report it under that representation, target, and time window; when the representation changes, redo the comparison. How much value history has in assisting prediction, how history formed the current carriers, and whether a historical-increment test in the narrow sense receives support are three questions that can have different answers. Appendix C.11 gives an exact example: the current projection has omissions, and historical gains arise as a result.

## 4.6 Representational Closure and Object or Intervention Conversion (G4) {#section-4-6}

This kind of question is called scale closure for short. A scale-closure test has two subtypes, and it likewise must preselect, before results appear, only one subtype and one success criterion for that subtype (§4.1); only when the preselected criterion exceeds the predetermined threshold or tolerance is the corresponding test supported. The two subtypes answer different questions.

The closure-failure subtype tests whether excluded scale variables still provide a conditional increment given the variables retained at the target scale. It permits three success criteria: “conditional-increment information,” “conditional predictive gain,” and “conditional intervention gain”; each test may preselect only one. Its null model is that, under the preselected criterion, the excluded scale variables provide no conditional increment beyond the threshold given the retained variables.

The subtype for object or intervention conversion tests whether the object or the intervention is converted under scale transformation (§3.4). It permits five success criteria: “noncommutation of object dynamics,” “noncommutation of intervention,” “failure of the identity criterion,” “change in effective relations,” and “change in intervention response”; each test likewise may preselect only one. The first two criteria say that scale transformation does not commute with object dynamics or with intervention, that is, a different order of the two gives a different result; the third says that the target mapping does not satisfy the identity criterion fixed in advance. The null model is also written only for the selected item: object dynamics commute, or interventions commute, or the target mapping satisfies the identity criterion fixed in advance, or effective relations do not change beyond tolerance, or intervention responses do not change beyond tolerance. The five must not be combined into a fallback formulation under which “any one holding counts.”

General correlation, unconditional coupling, similar narratives, and omitted variables at the same scale are all insufficient to support a positive conclusion from a scale-closure test. The test is suspended if exactly one subtype and success criterion were not preselected before results, or if any of the following was not fixed in advance: the mapping required by scale transformation and by the epistemic constraint “Testing Cross-Scale Transfer” (§4.8.5), the comparison models, the positive threshold, the null-conclusion rule, the requirements for statistical power or sensitivity, and the tolerance. When the positive criterion passes, the closure-failure subtype determines only closure failure within the pre-registered variables, metrics, scales, and time window; the subtype for object or intervention conversion determines only the preselected conversion candidate within the pre-registered mapping, identity criterion, scales, and time window. When the positive criterion does not pass, the default record is only “not supported or unresolved.” Only when the equivalence or sufficiency test, the requirements for statistical power or sensitivity, and the tolerance in the null-conclusion rule actually pass may the closure-failure subtype determine “closure within the limits of the criterion,” and the subtype for object or intervention conversion determine “the preselected conversion was not detected.” Merely being “not statistically significant,” or not passing the positive threshold, does not permit direct determination of closure, absence of object conversion, or equivalence of interventions. Unselected criteria must not be used for rescue, nor for refutation. Passing a scale-closure test does not mean that higher scales are truer, more important, or have greater authority to act.

This kind of question maintains a symmetrical research interest in closure and nonclosure. Supporting closure within a specified scope under a stated test can show that a concise representation is already sufficient for the current task; detecting nonclosure shows that the representation, task, or scope of operations needs revision. “Supported” and “null conclusion supported” are only evidential states relative to the preselected factual assertion, not rankings of the quality of a method or of theoretical progress.

Comparisons of scale closure must specify the targets, horizons, environments, and admissible operations relative to which closure is assessed. Sufficiency for prediction under ordinary conditions does not guarantee continued sufficiency under a new intervention; the same macro-level state may respond differently to the same operation because of differences in the composition collapsed by the representation. After the representation is found to be insufficient, necessary variables may be added, composition or history retained, the set of actions limited, the horizon shortened, or the use narrowed; there is no need to recover all micro-level details. Closure holding can itself be a successful result of an effective macro-level description; emergence, aggregation, omissions at the same scale, and cross-boundary transmission do not universally require closure failure.

Example of intervention sufficiency: two micro-level states A₁ and A₂ both have probability 0.2 of entering B under natural evolution, so merging them into A may be sufficient for this predictive task. Under a particular intervention, their probabilities of entering B are 0.8 and 0.1, respectively. The macro-level intervention outcome then depends on the share p of A₁, with probability 0.1+0.7p. One can then try adding only p rather than recovering all micro-level details. This teaching construction distinguishes predictive closure from intervention closure; it does not establish that real-world systems necessarily take these values. 〔Study 05.06〕

### 4.6.1 Formal Closure, Approximate Closure, and Empirical Eligibility {#section-4-6-1}

In a deterministic model, let the source state space be X, the representation be π:X→Y with Y=π(X), and the one-step dynamics be F:X→X. A macro-level update F̄:Y→Y satisfying π∘F=F̄∘π exists if and only if any two source states with the same representation still have the same representation after updating. This condition is an exact closure criterion within the model. If it holds throughout the state domain, commutation over multiple steps under fixed dynamics follows by induction; if it holds only at a few visited states, it cannot be extrapolated to closure over the entire domain. Sufficiency for a static query cannot replace this dynamic condition, either.

When multiple operations are admissible, the specific version of each operation should be stated and its update under state projection tested. If a macro-level operation does not determine its corresponding micro-level implementation, it is also necessary to check whether the various admissible implementations yield identical macro-level responses or responses within tolerance. A stochastic model must test the conditional transition probabilities to each target state or target block; identical means alone are insufficient to establish closure of the distribution. Adding a particular composition proportion can repair a one-step prediction at a specified time, but does not automatically supply an update law for that proportion itself, nor closure under every operation.

Approximate closure must be stated together with an error quantity, initial conditions, targets, an operation set, and a horizon. Suppose the per-step error under state projection is at most ε, and the Lipschitz constant of the proposed macro-level update on the relevant region does not exceed L. Starting from the same initial representation, the error at step t is then at most `ε∑_{j=0}^{t−1}L^j`; this bound requires the premises to hold at every step. When L&lt;1, a uniform upper bound ε/(1−L) is available; when L=1, the bound is tε; when L&gt;1, the error bound may increase rapidly. A one-step mean error or a short-term fitting error must not be substituted directly into this state-by-state, step-by-step bound.

These proofs provide structure under stated conditions; they do not provide support for any real-world scale-closure test. Measurement, mapping, samples, operations, and error conditions must still be checked empirically, with testing carried out separately against the positive criterion and the null-conclusion criterion. A formal proof of exact closure and failure to detect a deviation in limited data are evidence of two different statuses; without sufficient sensitivity, the latter remains unresolved.

</div>

## 4.7 A Fictional Set of Formal Tests {#section-4-7}

The following four small examples serve only to show how pre-registration works. They describe a fictional transport model with multiple layers, cite no external observations, and constitute no empirical support for any proposition of the four empirical questions.

Example one (grouping-gain test, predictive-gain subtype). The candidate object consists of several interconnected groups of units. The boundary, state variables, time window, scale profile, identity criterion, grouping method, and a null model of comparable complexity are fixed in advance; only “out-of-sample predictive gain” is selected as the success criterion, and its metric and threshold are fixed. If the candidate grouping is effective only in the training sample, or if the gain disappears after a slight boundary perturbation, example one fails; limited grouping-gain support within this time window is obtained only if the result is also reproduced in independent time blocks.

Example two (channel-effect test, controlled-identification subtype). For example one's candidate object, a transmission channel and a target state transition are specified in advance, only “controlled-perturbation effect” is selected as the success criterion, and the channel is then blocked under controlled conditions. Even if the blockage changes the target transition by more than the threshold, the channel can be determined only as a candidate for causal carrying. Suppose only delay is measured in this example; then capacity, reliability, controllability, and loss remain unknown and cannot be established along with example two.

Example three (historical-increment test, predictive path dependence). First define the historical variable “backlog in the preceding three rounds,” select only “historical conditional predictive gain” as the success criterion, then control for the current queue, environmental inputs, and measurement protocol. If the historical term still provides an out-of-sample conditional increment for the next round's outcome, predictive path dependence may be determined; without an intervention for history erasure or history recovery, this must not be raised to causal path dependence.

Example four (scale-closure test, closure-failure subtype). Before results, select only the closure-failure subtype and only “conditional-increment information” as the success criterion; fix in advance the micro-level and aggregated scales, the scale transformation between them, the retained variables, the excluded variables, the metric, and the threshold. Closure failure within this time window is determined only if the excluded local temporal sequence, given the aggregate variables, provides a conditional increment above the threshold under that criterion. A predictive or intervention gain must not be substituted after results are seen, nor may the result be rewritten as object conversion under the subtype for object or intervention conversion.

These examples show that the four tests can raise further questions for one another, but cannot replace one another. Passing example one does not guarantee that example two's channel is effective; passing example two does not guarantee example three's historical effect; and passing example three does not guarantee example four's scale-closure failure.

### 4.8.1 Constraint on Object Declaration (E1) {#section-4-8-1}

For the current judgment, this constraint requires the object, scope, scale, time window, necessary variables, and the object's identity conditions to be made clear; comparative claims must also specify the corresponding baseline or null model. When an item is missing, state which inferred conclusion it limits and how; independently established source facts must not be canceled merely because an item is missing from the format. When the seven elements of a structural object (§3.1) are incomplete, the analyst's grouping must not be declared an objective structure. Ordinary facts, textual meanings, and mathematical proofs are still reviewed under their own evidence and burden of argument.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="4.8.1" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 4.8.2 Observation Position and Model Limits (E2) {#section-4-8-2}

Observation positions, measurement channels, and model choices limit what is visible; one record must not be treated as the object's entire state. Nor does this constraint presuppose that every specific task must add perspectives. Whether a representation is sufficient depends on the target, horizon, environment, and admissible operations. Invisibility is not the same as nonexistence. When blind spots relevant to the task are substantial, evidence should be added, the use narrowed, or the judgment kept unresolved; whether probing is possible still requires comparing risks, time windows, and authorization.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="4.8.2" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 4.8.3 Conditional Observational Participation (E3) {#section-4-8-3}

Only when observation, naming, scoring, or publication enters into actual causal coupling with the object does it count as observational participation. Temporal order alone, or a mechanism story that “being observed changes the object,” is insufficient to pass this constraint; a specific channel must be located, and a counterfactual comparison established: what happens without observation, what happens under a different observation, or what happens when the channel is blocked.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="4.8.3" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 4.8.4 Retaining Competing Explanations and Residuals (E4) {#section-4-8-4}

In an explanation, competing accounts that the materials permit and that are sufficient to change the judgment should be compared, and counterexamples and unexplained residuals retained. What the competing explanations dispute must be specified: the size of an effect, its mechanism, contextual meaning, the scope of extrapolation, or normative premises. In the comparison, the evidential requirements, judgments about sources, and tests used to support or weaken one explanation must be applied equally to the explanations competing with it, including the explanation the analyst favors and explanations written in this framework’s terms. Where the standard has been relaxed for one side, or that side has been allowed to add auxiliary conditions to accommodate exceptions, the other sides must be given the same latitude; the added conditions must themselves have independent grounds and must not be added merely to explain a known failure (§16.3). Where the threshold for accepting materials of the same kind differs, a reason that does not depend on the conclusion to be established must be stated, for example that the two kinds of reports differ in how hard they are to observe, or that the conclusions differ in use and risk (§1.5.2). This requirement constrains the standard; it does not require the sides to reach the same conclusion, nor that their evidence carry equal weight.

Compatible explanations can each bear responsibilities at different layers. When several compatible explanations together account for a set of phenomena, state which phenomena and conditions each bears; a counterexample must not be reassigned to another explanation after the fact, and the baseline of comparison must not move with the results.

Whether or not an explanation is compatible with others, its failure conditions must be able to occur under the rules of determination or interpretation actually adopted. If an explanation reads opposite outcomes, and both the assent and the denial of the persons concerned, as support, then none of these outcomes and statements can be counted as support for it; this does not entail that the explanation is false, nor that the denial of the persons concerned is counterevidence. The analyst must separately state an observation that is obtainable in reality and is not reinterpreted under the same rule, as its failure condition; where none can be stated and the explanation is used for a strong judgment, the item “contrary conditions” in the strong-judgment kit is treated as missing (§13.5).

When no reasonable competing explanation is available, one must not be fabricated merely to fill a quota, but the scope of the search and failure conditions must still be stated. Openness, love, complexity, or a sense of theoretical wholeness cannot fill gaps in evidence. How competing explanations that have not yet been distinguished limit action must be judged in conjunction with harm, reversibility, external obligations, and current permissions.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="4.8.4" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 4.8.5 Testing Cross-Scale Transfer (E5) {#section-4-8-5}

Cross-scale transfer must pass the tests that scale transformation (§3.4) requires: of the mapping, invariants, changed items, lost items, error, competing explanations, and residuals. The only inferential premise of this constraint is a single definition, that of scale transformation. The two epistemic constraints “Constraint on Object Declaration” (§4.8.1) and “Retaining Competing Explanations and Residuals” (§4.8.4), together with the conditions for evidence (§2.5) and the conditions for analogy (§2.6), are all method requirements here: they do not enter the acyclic graph that inferential premises form (§2.3), nor masquerade as additional empirical causes. This constraint does not depend on the proposition of scale closure being true; conversely, every scale-closure test must pass this constraint. Expansion of observational scope does not imply expansion of authorization scope; the axis of jurisdiction and authorization must not be extrapolated automatically along with the other scale axes.

</div>

## 4.9 Evidential Status of Human-Experience Clauses {#section-4-9}

The three human-domain clauses “Meaning and Coordination,” “Power, Mediation, and Reflexivity,” and “Historical Trace Carriers” (§7.1) are empirical hypotheses awaiting testing. The specific factual assertions for all three are tested against their objects and evidence. Under this procedure, eligibility for formal support belongs only to specific tests that have completed the pre-registration and outcome record under §4.2 and met the corresponding criteria; definitions, the descriptions of the human variables, and the framework's own formulations are not external empirical support. Without eligible records of external tests, the record of empirical evidence is empty, and the evidential status is “awaiting evidence from a specific test.”

Thus, “Meaning and Coordination” needs to be tested case by case: preselect only one outcome family among resources, action, or coordination, and test the differences that meaning arrangements make. “Power, Mediation, and Reflexivity” also needs to be tested case by case: preselect only one outcome family among evidence coverage, safety of expression, object behavior, or reflexive response, then establish the channel through which position, mediation, or disclosure conditions act, together with the counterfactual comparison. “Historical Trace Carriers” must preselect only one carrier family, one specific carrier within it, and one durability criterion. Only if the family to which that carrier belongs matches the selected subtype, the historical event, baseline, trace observables, threshold, and durability time window were all fixed before results, and the selected criterion has passed, may candidate traces within the specified carrier and time window be determined, and the predefined historical variable be submitted as a candidate to a historical-increment test.

Historical conditional increments beyond the current representation, and the corresponding repair window, are handled separately by a historical-increment test and the conclusion rule “Historical Effects, Path Differences, and Repair Windows” (§4.12.1). Historical effects transmitted by current carriers and general effects of intervention timing obtain independent grounds under §4.5 and §6.10, respectively. Protection, harm reduction, or repair actions separately go through the conclusion rule “Normative Bridging” (§4.14.1), explicitly stated normative premises, and operational procedures. Clause names, internal definitions, or this framework's own narrative cannot complete the specific tests on behalf of these three clauses.

## 4.10 The Twelve Conclusion Rules (C1–C12) {#section-4-10}

Each conclusion rule delimits which conclusions can be drawn according to its own definitions, empirical basis, additional conditions, or normative and procedural premises; those of its branches that rely on formal tests must obtain the support of the corresponding tests. Each rule below is written under the same items: Grounds, Conditions, Can conclude, Cannot conclude, and Must also meet. “Grounds” lists the inferential premises, that is, the hard premises that enter the inferential acyclic graph (§2.3). “Must also meet” lists the method requirements, which determine whether a conclusion may be released but do not serve as empirical causes. Domain restrictions and objects of application are explained separately in the body. Minimum evidence, key counterexamples, and failure boundaries are presented together, group by group, in the table after each group.

The twelve conclusion rules (§4.11 to §4.14) do different argumentative work. In four places the work is mainly to record the outcomes within specified limits already supported by the corresponding empirical tests, adding a scope of use: the three rules “Grouping Gain and Its Robustness to Boundary Perturbation,” “Carrying and Channel Constraints,” and “Scale Closure and Object Conversion,” and the historical-increment route of “Historical Effects, Path Differences, and Repair Windows.” Six rules must also combine separately listed mechanism, temporal, or domain conditions: “Effective Feedback and Feedback-Mediated Learning,” “Load, Capacity, and Recovery,” “Local Improvement and Cross-Boundary Spillover,” “Observation Channels and Reflexive Responses,” “Human Uptake and Intertemporal Reproduction,” and “Institutional Write-Back.” The basic pattern of “Variation–Differential Retention–Reproduction” depends on separately obtained evidence of variation, differential retention, and reproduction. “Normative Bridging” is a check on normative procedure. Placing these in one sequence does not turn them into empirical laws of the same kind; nor does the same material, used in a formal test and cited again in a conclusion rule, constitute a second piece of independent support.

### 4.13.1 Variation–Differential Retention–Reproduction (C8) {#section-4-13-1}

## 4.16 Scope and Stopping Points of Empirical Judgments {#section-4-16}

Empirical judgments provide descriptions and limited explanations, constrained by the object, scale, time window, evidence, and extrapolation unit. Specific factual assertions under the four empirical questions must be tested through specific tests; conclusion rules record existing results or combine mechanism conditions; conditional formal propositions provide proofs under formal premises; and real-world mechanisms still require independent case evidence. Definitions, identifiers, examples, and the framework's internal descriptions cannot determine whether an empirical proposition holds in the world. ### 4.17.4 Method Effectiveness and Evaluation of the Framework {#section-4-17-4}

The four empirical questions organize empirical testing; their number does not establish sufficient coverage. The twelve conclusion rules organize the use of results; their completeness does not establish real-world reliability. Testing whether the entire framework is worth using should involve a separate comparison with common targets, common materials, and a common budget: compared with an adequate specialist method or a simpler approach, whether the framework reduces substantive errors, preserves necessary distinctions, and improves explanation or decision-making, and whether its costs are proportionate. Without such a comparison, only its intended uses and the local results already checked may be stated. In that case, the evidential status of the framework’s net gain on the task concerned is “awaiting evidence from comparison”; the number of times it has been used, users’ approval, and the number of studies written with this framework do not change that status. Studies written with the help of a tool based on this framework (for example an AI tool) are not themselves such a comparison: they can supply records of misuse and candidates awaiting testing; when such studies are the party being evaluated in a comparison, the evaluation standards, the comparator, and the reviewers must be independent of the tool and of its developers and maintainers. The effect in a single test, the rate at which one of the empirical questions applies within a particular set of objects, and the framework's net gain on a specified task are three different empirical targets. Sampling units, scopes of extrapolation, and evaluation rules must each be stated; a batch of successful cases or a large volume of reading materials must not be treated as proof of universal effectiveness. To evaluate what this framework added in a given analysis, take as the comparator an equally careful specialist practice or close reading that does not use this framework’s terms; the comparator must have actually been carried out or be open to checking: distinctions that the work being read, the domain method, or close reading could have obtained anyway are not recorded as the framework’s gain (§2.7.1). ### 4.18.1 Task Recovery (F1) {#section-4-18-1}

Let X be a nonempty state domain, with representation π:X→Y and Y=π(X), and target q:X→V. A decoder g:Y→V using only the representation and satisfying q=g∘π exists if and only if π(x)=π(x′) always implies q(x)=q(x′). When this condition holds, g is unique on Y. In other words, states for which the current task requires different answers cannot be merged by the same representation. See Appendix C.6 for the proof and for bounds on scalar recovery error on a finite state domain.

</div>

<SectionLesson section="4.18.1" />

<div v-pre class="theory-source">


### 4.18.2 Closure under Dynamics and Admissible Interventions (F2) {#section-4-18-2}

Let F:X→X be deterministic one-step dynamics. A macro-level update F̄ satisfying π∘F=F̄∘π exists if and only if any two source states with the same representation still have the same representation after updating. If this condition holds throughout the state domain, evolution over any finite number of steps under fixed dynamics commutes. When the admissible actions form a set U, this condition must hold separately for every `F_u`. Check whether each action can be implemented across the relevant source states sharing a representation. Check the different micro-level implementations as well. Sufficiency for static recovery does not automatically satisfy closure under dynamics or closure under intervention; a stochastic model must also compare the complete transition probabilities to target blocks. See Appendix C.8 for the proof.

### 4.18.3 Common Feasible Actions under Information Constraints (F3) {#section-4-18-3}

For a finite, single-round deterministic task, let the current materials be compatible with a nonempty set of states `X_y`, and let each state x have a set of eligible actions `A_ok(x)`, already determined according to the target, protections, resources, and valid permissions. An action based only on the current representation y that qualifies in every compatible state exists if and only if `∩_{x∈X_y}A_ok(x)` is nonempty. Identifying every state is therefore not a universal prerequisite for bounded action, while the availability of a solution in each state separately does not guarantee that the right choice can be made now. See Appendix C.12 for the proof; randomized strategies and sequential strategies must establish their own conditions.

### 4.18.4 Task Preservation through Successive Conversions (F4) {#section-4-18-4}

Let f:X→Z and h:Z→W, with the first stage satisfying q=g∘f. The composition h∘f can still recover the same final target q if and only if g is constant on every fiber of h restricted to the actual image f(X). The individual steps complete different local tasks; this cannot replace the condition at the shared endpoint. Preservation under a functional representation, causal effects connected in sequence, and joint implementation using shared resources need to be checked separately; a single mark reading “connected” cannot substitute for these different checks. See Appendix C.7 and C.12 for the proof and counterexamples.

### 4.18.5 Error Propagation under Approximate Closure (F5) {#section-4-18-5}

Let the target space have a metric d. Assume that the projection error is no greater than ε at every state reached at each step, and that the Lipschitz constant of the proposed macro-level update on the relevant region is no greater than L. Starting from the same initial representation, the error after t steps satisfies `e_t≤ε∑_{j=0}^{t−1}L^j`. This bound requires the error condition and the region condition to continue to hold at every step; mean fitting error cannot directly replace a state-by-state bound. When L&lt;1, there is an upper bound ε/(1−L); when L=1, it is tε; when L&gt;1, the upper bound may increase rapidly. See Appendix C.8 for the proof.

## 4.19 Task Families and Dynamic Refinement {#section-4-19}

Which distinctions a structural representation should retain depends on the tasks at issue and the operations allowed to act on the object. A finer representation can preserve more differences and also increases measurement, storage, explanation, and maintenance costs; not every detail is a necessary input to every task. In a fully specified finite deterministic model, it is possible to state precisely which states can be merged and compute the coarsest partition satisfying particular requirements.

Let X be a nonempty finite state set, Q a finite set of target functions `q:X→V_q`, and U a finite set of admissible actions. Each u∈U corresponds to a total deterministic update `F_u:X→X`; the actions are implementable in every state, and no external disturbance that has not been stated is introduced here. The empty action string denotes no update. For an action string `w=u₁…u_k`, write `F_w=F_{u_k}∘…∘F_{u₁}`. These conditions delimit the objects of the proposition below; they cannot be assumed satisfied merely because real-world operations share a name with it.

**Proposition F6 The Coarsest Sufficient Partition for a Task Family.** For a nonnegative integer H, define `x∼_H x′` if and only if, for every q∈Q and every admissible action string w of length no greater than H, `q(F_w(x))=q(F_w(x′))`. This is an equivalence relation. The representation formed by its equivalence classes retains precisely the task answers within the given horizon. Any deterministic representation that answers these questions exactly may merge only states within the same equivalence class.

Proof: Reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity follow from the corresponding properties of equality of function values. The answer for every target and action string is constant within an equivalence class, so by proposition F1 it is uniquely determined by that class. If another representation merges two nonequivalent states, then by definition there must be a target and an admissible action string giving different answers; by proposition F1, that representation cannot recover both answers exactly. Thus, the partition induced by any sufficient representation is finer than or equal to this partition. This coarseness concerns a state partition relative to X, Q, U, and H; it does not establish the fewest parameters, the cheapest measurement, or the irreplaceability of a particular natural-language concept.

Sufficiency for a fixed finite horizon does not automatically produce dynamic closure for that same horizon. Take X=&#123;a,b,c,d&#125;, with the sole target q(a)=q(b)=q(c)=0 and q(d)=1, and the sole action satisfying F(a)=c, F(b)=b, F(c)=d, and F(d)=d. States a and b both give 0 now and after one step, so a∼₁b. Their successors c and b, however, give different answers after one further step, so c and b do not belong to the same ∼₁ class. For H≥1, what is always guaranteed is that `x∼_H x′` implies `F_u(x)∼_{H−1}F_u(x′)`. When advancing, the remaining horizon should be reduced accordingly, or closure of the fixed partition under admissible actions must be required separately.

To preserve every target under admissible action strings of any finite length, define `x∼_*x′` to hold when the answers agree for all such finite strings. This partition is closed under each `F_u`: prepending u to any subsequent string gives a string already compared in the definition. Conversely, any representation that recovers all targets in Q and is closed under all `F_u` can be advanced step by step and used to recover the targets after any finite string, so the partition it induces must be finer than or equal to `∼_*`. Thus `∼_*` is the unique coarsest state partition satisfying these conditions; uniqueness is determined by the equivalence classes, irrespective of their labels.

On a finite domain, this partition can be computed directly. Begin by grouping states by their values on all current targets to form P₀. Each round uses only the preceding partition `P_h` and splits its blocks synchronously by the signature `([x]_{P_h},([F_u(x)]_{P_h})_{u∈U})` to obtain `P_{h+1}`. Here `[x]_{P_h}` denotes the block containing x in the preceding partition. Each strict refinement adds at least one block, so at most `|X|−1` strict refinements can occur. Once no block splits further, the blocks preserve the current targets and satisfy closure under each action. Level H of synchronous iteration corresponds exactly to target comparisons for strings of length at most H, so the stable partition equals `∼_*`. With asynchronous refinement one block at a time, final stability can still be checked. Its operation count must not be used as the horizon H here. This procedure is computable on a complete finite table. It does not guarantee identification of true states from incomplete observations, much less tractability for large models within a given budget.

This result gives a conditional rule for simplification. Merge states that cannot be distinguished by the given targets and admissible operations. Retain a distinction only when a task or an operation actually distinguishes the states. Adding targets or admissible actions leaves the required partition unchanged or refines it. Shortening the horizon, narrowing the use, or restricting the operations can make a simpler representation sufficient. Adjusting the task can yield a legitimate new question, but the adjustment does not warrant declaring the original task complete.

Error tolerances require separate analysis. The condition “the two answers differ by no more than ε” is generally not transitive. The values 0 and ε are close, and ε and 2ε are close, but 0 and 2ε may differ by more than the tolerance. Approximate similarity therefore cannot directly replace the equivalence relation above or automatically yield a unique coarsest partition. One may use a bound on block diameter, worst-case recovery error, or a specified probabilistic loss. The optimal solution, stability, and computational cost for each require separate proof.

This section states a proposition about sets and dynamics in a finite model. Distinguishing states by input strings, forming equivalence classes, and refining them recursively have a direct mathematical connection to Moore’s work on sequential machines. The preservation of states and actions also connects to abstraction in controlled dynamics. The finite model stated in this section and its proof establish the conclusion without relying on additional optimality results for other classes of machines. 〔Moore, 1956, pp. 143 and 145–146; Ravindran and Barto, 2003〕 This section neither declares that nature has a single correct hierarchy nor reduces people’s legitimate claims to the current predictive targets. Which targets must be preserved, which actions may be implemented, and what error is acceptable still have to be determined separately on the basis of domain facts, normative reasons, and real-world permissions. When these givens are challenged, they are handled under §2.14.
