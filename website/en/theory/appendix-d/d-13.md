# D.13 Kinship, Exchange, Ritual, Ethnography, and Cultural Change {#section-d-13}

In the main text, see §2.5–2.7, §7.5, §9.7, §10, and §16.6. Research entries cover 13.01–13.08.

Kinship rules, gifts and debts, rituals and classifications, habitus and capital, ecology and livelihoods, and ethnography each retain their own units of analysis. Nonmarket exchange does not entail an absence of obligations. Gifts and commodities can also be intertwined within one relationship. Ritual coordination, public identity, private belief, and long-term cooperation can be out of sync. Attending together cannot establish all those outcomes.

Symbolic interpretation should explain how a practice is understood in context. Material and institutional research traces resources, labor, constraints, and consequences. These approaches can check each other without substituting for each other. Habitus can develop stable tendencies through long practice and can also change through multiple experiences of socialization and changes in the environment. Conversion of capital requires recognition and rules within a particular field. It does not operate as an unconditional transfer of energy.

Access for ethnographic work, trust, recording, publication, and authority to represent people each require their own grounds. The detail of thick description can show researchers experiences a questionnaire misses. Familiarity with a field does not give them knowledge of every member’s wishes. How the observer’s presence changes expression, who was not contacted, and who cannot speak safely should all remain part of the explanation. Calling the material “subjective” cannot justify dismissing it all.

In a teaching example, a festival introduces a new medium, changing participation and income. Some skills are preserved, while other experiences no longer occur. Genuine continuing cultural creation can coexist with definite losses. “New creation” does not amount to faking a tradition. “Preserving tradition” does not establish the absence of internal control or problems of distribution. Disagreements between cultural relativism and shared protections should be addressed in arguments about the particular case.

Strong commitments by original authors must be preserved as stated in their texts. When borrowing a local analysis of symbolic interpretation, material conditions, or conversion of capital, do not first reduce all its claims about priority, a common scale, or overall coordination to neutral reminders and then declare the traditions compatible. The presence of ritual, physiological synchrony, expressions of identity, accuracy of memory, and sustained cooperation each have their own units of evidence. Shared rituals and repeated use of the same underlying sample cannot count as several independent validations merely because the results appear in separate publications.

Research topics. 13.01 Kinship, exchange, and gifts (Mauss, Lévi-Strauss, and others). 13.02 Ritual, liminality, and a sense of commonality (Durkheim, van Gennep, and Victor Turner). 13.03 Symbols, classification, and interpretation of culture (Geertz and Douglas). 13.04 Habitus, field, and capital (Bourdieu). 13.05 Cultural materialism, ecology, and livelihoods. 13.06 Ethnography, thick description, and participant observation. 13.07 Cultural relativism and shared protections. 13.08 Cultural change, mixing, and revival.
