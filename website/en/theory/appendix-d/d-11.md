# D.11 Modern Reinterpretations of Chinese Thought and Traditions of Practice {#section-d-11}

In the main text, see §2.6–2.7, §9, §12, and §14. Research entries cover 11.01–11.10.

Confucian accounts of roles, self-cultivation, and criticism raise different questions from Daoist accounts of *ziran* 自然 (naturalness), *qiwu* 齐物 (equalizing things), and the limits of knowledge. Mohist *jian ai* 兼爱 (impartial care), *fei gong* 非攻 (opposition to aggression), and practical efficacy raise further questions, as do Legalist rules, organization, and positional power, and military strategists’ attention to circumstances, information, and timing. Borrowing from these traditions should preserve the historical conditions and tensions within their texts. Their terms must not be reduced to synonyms for concepts the modern framework already has.

Role responsibilities can sustain care and can also be used to conscript it. Predictable rules can constrain local arbitrariness while central power remains unchecked. Less control can improve lives without implying that necessary support should be withdrawn. Effective military strategies provide no permission to move coercive methods into ordinary relationships. Different interpretations should be tested against specific objects and outcomes.

Sincerity does not guarantee an accurate factual judgment, and knowing a principle does not guarantee an opportunity to implement it. Other people’s willingness to listen is a separate matter from whether institutions let dissent safely change a decision. In a teaching example, an increase in local supplies on the books can support a claim about changes in inventory. Judging residents’ circumstances still requires tracing who is eligible, what they actually receive, its quality, and control within households. Calling the policy *minben* 民本 (taking the people as the foundation) cannot substitute for those checks.

*Lixue* 理学 (the learning of principle) and *xinxue* 心学 (the learning of the mind), *jingshi* 经世 (statecraft) and institutional practice, interpretations of *shiwei* 时位 (timing and position) in the *Zhouyi* 周易, and transformations in modern Chinese thought require separate treatment of theoretical principles, historical facts, methodological insights, and normative reasons. Insights from *tongbian* 通变 (understanding and adapting to change) do not automatically entail accepting the premises of divination. Modern interpretations in terms of equality or subjecthood also do not establish that earlier authors held exactly the same concepts. Traditions can be criticized, reinterpreted, and developed further. The framework cannot end the debate by calling them “compatible.”

An institution in a tradition can use different arrangements for eligibility, examinations, appointments, and actual opportunities to enter. Equality in one dimension does not establish equality overall. A difference in one dimension does not establish differences in the same direction at every step. Written rules, local implementation, and the experience of people affected support distinct conclusions. Modern reinterpretation can favor equality and independent review, but must acknowledge these as present normative choices. They cannot be written back as the entirety of the earlier authors’ meaning.
