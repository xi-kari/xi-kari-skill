# D.05 Information, Representation, and Borrowing From Mathematics and Physics {#section-d-05}

In the main text, see §2.6 and 2.8, §4.6 and 4.8, §5, and Appendix C. Research entries cover 05.01–05.08.

A representation must be evaluated relative to the question. Whether source states can be recovered, the specified task can be completed, computing costs are bearable, future dynamics have closure, and the representation remains sufficient after permitted interventions are five distinct dimensions. Losing source information need not cause the current task to fail. Sufficiency for one task cannot be extrapolated to all future questions.

Formally, a function g satisfying q=g∘f exists if and only if q takes the same value on every preimage of an individual value under f. This is a criterion for representational sufficiency. Actual computability is judged separately. The criterion gives a positive condition for abstraction to work in addition to warning about losses. If only one hidden share affects the outcome after intervention, that share can be added. Failure of closure need not lead to an endless attempt to recover every detail. Actual adoption should also account for the costs of measurement, privacy, maintenance, and understanding.

Unit conversions, accounting identities, empirical conversion rates, and value weights are established separately. Conservation of energy or matter requires appropriate boundaries and dimensions. Social burdens, suffering, attention, and moral responsibility do not acquire a conservation law by sharing the word “load.” Eliminating duplicated work, improving technology, or reducing harm can genuinely lower costs. It need not mean that costs were shifted elsewhere.

Results concerning Gödel, incompleteness, and computability depend on particular formal systems and kinds of problem. They do not imply that every finite task is undecidable. Model parsimony also depends on more than parameter count. Comparisons should include the full selection process, representations searched, tuning budget, and changes in objectives. Results in physics or mathematics can supply precise rules for bridging. Their prestige cannot substitute for empirical premises in the target domain.

Successive conversions must keep the same final question. If the first step is sufficient, the second must still preserve the distinctions needed for the final answer among the intermediate representations actually reachable. Completing two different local tasks separately does not establish that their composition still answers the original question. Check separately whether the answer function exists, whether an effective algorithm exists, whether it can be computed within budget, whether material is available in time, and whether actual use is correct. A complete finite table can be looked up. On a general infinite domain, preservation of information does not imply an algorithm that always halts.
