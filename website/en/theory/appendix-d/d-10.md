# D.10 Ethical Disagreement, Capabilities, and Bounded Responsibility {#section-d-10}

In the main text, see §14, §15.2, and §16.2. Research entries cover 10.01–10.08.

Virtue ethics emphasizes practical judgment and how an actor lives. Deontology attends to constraints against treating people merely as means. Consequentialism compares the benefits and harms an action causes. Each tradition also has its own internal disputes. The framework should bring the reasons to bear on the same specific problem. It should not declare that one theory prevails in every task. The same facts can leave genuine normative disagreements. There is no need to invent empirical effects to eliminate them.

Resources, achievable capabilities, choices, and actual living outcomes each have their own conditions of conversion. With the same amount of money, people in different circumstances of care, disability, transport, and institutions may have different opportunities. Several capabilities that can each be maximized separately may not all be realizable together. Comparisons should retain genuinely feasible combinations. Being willing to accept, being able to afford it, and having no other option are also distinct conditions.

Adding utilities across people requires disclosing scales and weights. The possibility of compensation does not amount to compensation actually delivered. Freedom can be understood in terms of the extent of current intervention or evaluated by whether someone is subject to arbitrary power. The latter is a normative choice that must be explained. It is not a physical fact. Cultural differences do not automatically cancel shared protections. Claims for protection must also address different ways of living and the costs of implementation.

Responsibility should distinguish attribution of conduct, fault, causal contribution, benefit, preventive capability, and remedy. An institution’s ability to help does not make its duties unlimited. Lack of fault also need not rule out a remedy with independent grounds. A tragic choice may have no option without loss. Fully recording the requirements left unmet and the responsibilities that follow makes accountability possible in a way that deleting the costs from the metrics does not.

In a teaching example, a care facility has only one care worker on duty at night, two residents need help at the same time, delay carries an irreversible risk for both, and there is no option without loss. The record should state the decision rule relied on, for example attending first to the resident whose danger is more imminent. The other resident’s claim still holds, and the facility must afterward explain and provide remedy. The record must also state who decided on the constraint of staffing only one person at night and whether it should be reconsidered. The loss to the resident attended later cannot be recorded as zero, nor can it be used to conclude that the care worker failed in their duty.

Under the same facts, an aggregate principle and independent constraints concerning basic standing can permit different ranges of action. The analysis should disclose this normative disagreement. It must not use unsupported claims about future collapse to establish that every arrangement lacking justification is necessarily inefficient. A weighted sum can represent outcomes mathematically without determining units across people, social weights, or which losses may be offset. Bounded procedural duties can be fulfilled while irreversible losses in people’s lives remain.
