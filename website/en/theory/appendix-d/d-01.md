# D.01 The Framework Itself, Explanatory Tasks, and Borrowing From Theory {#section-d-01}

In the main text, see §1.3–1.10, §2.2–2.7, and §16.15–16.16. Research entries cover 01.01–01.08.

Start by asking which outcome needs explaining. Do not begin with whichever vocabulary is most familiar. Why equipment failed, why a decision took shape, who bears the consequences, and how the people concerned understand what they experienced are related but distinct tasks. Technical mechanisms, institutional authority, and lived experience can correct one another or each provide the main account of a particular part of the problem. Structure, interests, power, psychology, culture, and technology should not have permanent priority before evidence is examined.

Borrowing from theory requires distinguishing at least guidance for observation, definitions, testable mechanisms, formal inferences, and normative choices. The same term appearing in two theories does not guarantee that the objects, variables, or permitted inferences are the same. A formal mapping should state which relation it preserves, which distinctions it loses, and what circumstances would change the conclusion. If a domain method already answers the question sufficiently, the framework can limit its role to checking sources and boundaries. It need not introduce additional circles into the analysis.

In a teaching example, an automatic reminder reduces missed tasks, but appeals still cannot change errors. Improvement on the technical task can coexist with inadequate procedural remedy. To ask whether users are treated with respect, one also needs evidence of their actual circumstances and what they say. Calling all three outcomes “a better system” would obscure the differences. It would likewise be a mistake to let a shortcoming in one invalidate all the gains.

Keep historical adoption of a theory, mention in a catalog, compatibility discovered later, and new connections proposed by an analyst separate. Versions in different texts do not have the same identity merely because they share a number. Obtaining one manuscript cannot supply wording in another source that has yet to be checked. Successful procedural checks, complete files, and a closed numbering scheme establish only the corresponding facts about the engineering work. They do not establish that the meanings are correct or that use in reality improves outcomes.

An analysis can yield answers at the conceptual, textual, formal, domain-empirical, and practical levels in succession. The first four answers, where established, do not disappear because the last has yet to be obtained. Clearer writing or more material cannot stand in for practical benefits. To judge the value of an added method, fix the actual material processed, the task, and comparable resources. Retain a strong domain method as a comparison and account for undelivered work, misjudgments, waiting, and maintenance burden.
