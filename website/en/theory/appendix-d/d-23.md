# D.23 Family, Gender, the Body, Dependence, and Care {#section-d-23}

In the main text, see §9.7–9.8, §10.5–10.8, §14.3, and §15.3.1–15.3.2. Research entries cover 23.01–23.08.

Marriage, kinship, co-residence, intimacy, property rights, care, and statutory responsibilities are distinct. A family’s total resources do not establish that each member can make decisions and use them. Remittances across locations also do not automatically substitute for daily care. Research on patriarchy, social reproduction, feminist traditions, and work and family each retains its own analysis of labor, the body, rules, and experience.

Care burdens include time spent on care as the main activity, simultaneous tasks, planning and arranging, being on call, and effects on sleep and future opportunities. Counting paid working hours alone is insufficient. To assess how career returns depend on continuity at work and flexibility of time, examine job design, employer constraints, public support, and discrimination. Observed pay differences do not automatically establish natural ability or a single cause.

Intimate conflict, ongoing control, and immediate danger are distinct situations. Communication between the parties or joint therapy is not suitable as a blanket response. Safe exit does not require ideal resources to be in place. Arrangements for continuing bounded responsibilities must not force people who have been harmed to remain exposed. A child’s expression, the weight given to their views, consent on a particular matter, and final decision-making authority should be kept separate. Provide support for understanding and expression first. Lack of independent expression must not be used to deny the help needed for it. See §16.12–16.13 for support roles and professional authority.

In a teaching example, an assistive arrangement enables a person who depends on others to participate in decisions, while they still need daily support. Dependence and autonomy can coexist. The ability to help does not make a helper’s obligations unlimited. Preserve experiences of the body, sexuality, illness, aging, and disability, as well as institutional barriers. Medical treatment, improvements in daily life, and administrative eligibility cannot establish one another. Specific professional and legal judgments require their own procedures.

Time saved in one household may depend on additional work by another household, grandparents, or paid caregivers. In evaluating long-term reductions in burden, retain where the burden goes and the risk of double-counting simultaneous activities, being on call, and coordination. Meaningful work can still be difficult to refuse. Kinship, households, residences, and care networks across households have different boundaries. Averages from two valid populations cannot be treated as paired records for spouses.
