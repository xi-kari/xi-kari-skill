# D.29 Cities, Space, Housing, Rural Areas, and Public Accessibility {#section-d-29}

In the main text, see §5.5.1–5.5.3, §10, and §15.3.7–15.3.8. Research entries cover 29.01–29.08.

Distinguish the material conditions for urban formation, historical paths, and current agglomeration benefits. Geographic location, administrative jurisdiction, membership, functional networks, and service areas also have distinct boundaries. When the same city ID is used across periods, check the statistical coverage and tracking rules. An unchanged ID does not establish a fixed spatial boundary (United Nations Population Division, 2025). Once connectivity is established on a map, actual access still depends on times of access, capacity, cost, eligibility, support needs, and alternative routes. Centrality does not directly establish power or welfare.

Everyday contact, use at staggered times, and familiarity with a place can support help-seeking and looking out for others. Safety, basic respect, and users’ ability to remain in place still require evidence. Local participation can also exclude renters, future residents, and users from other areas. Observe separately whether a space is visible, whether it can be used lawfully, whether affordable access is continuous, whether people can use it safely and autonomously, and whether people can change the rules under which it is managed.

Distinguish land rights, building rights, tenure security for renters, mortgages, eligibility to return, and maintenance costs. A neighborhood’s name and appearance do not by themselves establish an individual’s poverty, a violation of law, or eligibility for intervention (see Roy, 2005). When combining categories for a specific statistical use, retain the classification mapping and deduplicate the records. Rural areas support production, nonfarm work, care, and supply across places. They are not a less advanced stage of urban development. New roads can expand peripheral markets and can also intensify competition as firms from the center enter. Growing more slowly in relative terms does not amount to absolute decline.

Teaching example. A redeveloped neighborhood has a higher average income, but a different set of residents. The change in the current neighborhood can be real. It cannot stand in for outcomes in the lives of original households or those who moved away. After a disaster, track building safety and renewed exposure, services for households, relationship networks, local memory, feasible choices, and remedies separately. Moving is not necessarily bad, and rebuilding in place is not necessarily good. The observable pattern of partial recovery does not automatically establish a feedback trap.

Using a common list of cities at each time point addresses differences in the sample at that time only. A fixed statistical cohort requires holding the list fixed across all time points. Even a fixed cohort does not ensure that geometric boundaries or residents stay the same. Holding the historical area fixed and holding the current area fixed can serve different valid indexes. State the denominator explicitly. A facility’s service area is a spatial representation based on assumptions. Continuous routes, actual times of access, people’s physical circumstances, and autonomous use still require corresponding evidence.
