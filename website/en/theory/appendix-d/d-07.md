# D.07 Bounded Rationality, Emotion, Group Decisions, and Exploration {#section-d-07}

In the main text, see §9, §11.5–11.8, and §12. Research entries cover 07.01–07.08.

Bounded rationality first asks what information, time, attention, and resources for execution are available when people decide. Determining what is normatively optimal, describing actual choice, and recommending improvements are three distinct tasks. Framing effects, reference points, beliefs, probability weighting, and utility curvature cannot be replaced by the label “irrational.” Given the same likelihood information, different priors can reasonably produce different posteriors.

Professional intuition can be useful in environments with learnable regularities and effective feedback. Seniority and confidence alone do not establish accuracy. Emotions can carry clues about risk and express values, and they can affect the actions available. Calm is no proof of being right, and an intense reaction is no proof of being wrong. Memory, present preferences, real future constraints, and fatigue from execution need to be distinguished.

Group judgment must distinguish independence of members’ responses, independence of relationships, independence of factual sources, and independence of errors. A majority of majorities can differ from the majority of all individuals. Several opinions can also repeat the same source. In a teaching example, each subgroup contributes one representative vote. This compresses away differences in group size and variation within the groups. To choose an aggregation rule, first state the evaluation objective and what qualifies someone to represent a group. “Collective wisdom” cannot substitute for a rule.

A small exploratory step has positive value only if information can change an executable plan in time, risk is bearable, and stopping or substitution is genuinely feasible. The value of knowledge need not all be converted into gains from action, but an investigation undertaken for a decision must weigh the costs of delay and privacy. Past investments that cannot be recovered differ from relocation, care, and contract costs and retaliation risks still to be faced. They must not all be treated as sunk-cost bias.

The evidential value of a message depends on the structure of its sources and the process by which it was included. The same nine positive reports can be repetitions of one underlying source or come from several conditionally independent observations. Their value for social coordination may still change as the number of supporters grows. Information that is free to obtain and can be ignored, and that leaves the objective and action set unchanged, has nonnegative gross value under an optimal policy. Investigation costs, lost opportunities, loss of privacy, and incorrect use enter the actual net value.

Research topics. 07.01 Bounded rationality and satisficing (Simon). 07.02 Heuristics, prospect theory, and framing effects (Kahneman and Tversky). 07.03 Naturalistic decision-making and professional intuition. 07.04 Belief updating, motivated reasoning, and cognitive dissonance. 07.05 Emotion, risk perception, and bodily states. 07.06 Attention, memory, and time preferences. 07.07 Group decision-making, groupthink, and collective wisdom. 07.08 Reversible choices, exploration, and the value of information.
