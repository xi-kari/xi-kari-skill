# D.15 Games, Cooperation, Signals, Commitment, and Implementation {#section-d-15}

In the main text, see §10.5, §11.3 and 11.7, §12, and §14. Research entries cover 15.01–15.12.

A game representation starts by specifying actual decision nodes, information, feasible choices, outcomes, and evaluation. Being included among the players does not confer full authority to represent others. People outside the player set may still bear costs and have rights. A third party that can change permissions or resources should appear in the causal model. If it currently has no node at which it can exercise control, it still cannot be removed from the normative allocation.

Equilibrium existence, equilibrium selection, actual attainment, dynamic stability, efficiency, and normative justification each require their own grounds. Repeated interaction can sustain cooperation and can also undermine it through false alarms, short-term incentives, or uncontrolled punishment. A costly signal can distinguish types only if the recipient’s interpretation fits each type’s incentives to choose it and ability to bear its cost.

Distinguish the announcement of a commitment, reasons to honor it later, and the actual ability to honor it when the time comes. Deterrence differs from demanding reversal of an action already taken. Neither is shown to work by a forceful declaration alone. An assurance that the threat can end after compliance also matters. Truthful reporting in mechanism design does not automatically address hidden action, the incentives of overseers, multiple equilibria, or authority to implement.

In a teaching example, a rule gets members to report their needs truthfully but leaves oversight to people who benefit from violations. Reporting can work while implementation still fails. Revise the particular actions and chain of incentives. A general claim that rational models are useless does not identify what needs changing. The core, Shapley allocations, and bargaining solutions answer different questions about cooperation. They do not by themselves produce a unique fair allocation. An actual violation enters e(t). Simulating a violation does not confer permission to carry it out.

A guarantee about a mechanism must state the set of permitted actions. Actions permitted by the rules may differ from actions that can actually be taken. An unprofitable deviation in the former set does not show that violations in the latter are no longer attractive. A guarantee of truthful reporting also does not automatically cover hidden implementation, shared funding, or losses to third parties. After adding implementers and resource constraints, solve the corresponding mechanism again and retain the original guarantee within the scope of its original conditions.
