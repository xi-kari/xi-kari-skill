# D.31 Risk, Disasters, Public Health, Command, and Recovery {#section-d-31}

In the main text, see §6, §8.6, §12.6, §15.3.8, and §16.13. Research entries cover 31.01–31.11.

Hazard sources, exposure, vulnerability, observation, acceptance of risk, and action are distinct. Social conditions cannot erase the intensity of a flood or the presence of a pathogen. Technical estimates alone cannot determine who should bear the risk. Preventive measures should be compared in terms of the harms of action itself, proportionality, feasible support, and provision for review. Uncertainty does not justify an unlimited expansion of authority, and limited protective action need not wait for complete certainty.

Complex coupling, reliable organizational practices, latent lines of defense, system constraints, and routine task adaptations each have their own explanatory scope. A temporary workaround that retains checks is distinct from removing checks. Whether checks have been retained depends on whether the protective function and task-critical distinctions are still tested. The format can differ. A substitute verification procedure appropriate to the task must have a professional basis. The evidentiary value of observing no incidents depends on exposure and observation. A new process may actually have been established after an incident even if its net effect on safety is still undetermined and one branch of feedback-mediated learning still lacks support.

Critical slowing down applies only under the corresponding conditions of dynamics, noise, sampling, and bifurcation. Arbitrary fluctuations cannot be taken as signs of impending collapse. An early warning must reach an endpoint where the warning can be understood, a response can be organized, and protective action can be completed in time. An absence of harm after a warning may mean the measures worked. Infection, testing, confirmed diagnosis, reporting, and hospitalization have different denominators. Changes in beliefs, actually presenting for testing, and health outcomes cannot be inferred from one another.

Medical classification, lived experience, the course of illness, provision of benefits, and treatment choices each carry their own evidentiary burden. Shared decision-making can improve knowledge and does not automatically guarantee long-term health. Emergency command handoffs, coordinated demobilization, the end of special powers, and recovery of living conditions have different clocks. Functional recovery achieved with temporary support is real. A later shock does not necessarily show that the earlier conclusion was entirely wrong. Only recovery claims that exceed the evidence already available need to be narrowed accordingly.

Warning lead time must cover verification, reaching recipients, comprehension, mobilization, and the time it takes for protective measures to take effect. If labels are complete and the target has been fixed, the absence of a post-warning event can still be scored against that target. Estimating how much harm action prevented requires a separate causal comparison. Periods when monitoring is offline cannot be coded as zero events. Recovery of function with temporary power or ongoing support can be genuine recovery. The assessment must retain the support conditions and the population actually covered. It cannot automatically be extended to all pre-disaster users.
