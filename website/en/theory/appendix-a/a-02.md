# A.2 Boundaries and Interfaces (HV02) {#section-a-2}

1. Proposition and Scope of Application

Proposition: For human boundaries, members, resources, information, rights, responsibilities, and cross-boundary interfaces must be stated together.

Scope of application: Human structures with inclusion, exclusion, exchange, or jurisdiction.

Conditions for suspension: Formal and actual boundaries are conflated, or what is excluded is invisible.

2. Grounds and Limits on Conclusions

Inferential premises: Structural domain (§7.3.1).

Method requirements: Constraint on Object Declaration (§4.8.1); conditions for evidence (§2.5); conditions for sources (§2.7).

Domain restriction: None.

Objects of application: None.

Support routes:

Route one, boundary inventory. Conclusion level: Descriptive classification. Conditions: Members, resources, information, rights, responsibilities, cross-boundary interfaces, and the differences between formal and actual boundaries can be stated item by item. Further inferential premises: None. Further method requirements: None. Can conclude: Describe boundary states, interface accessibility, gatekeeping positions, records of refusal, and exclusion risks. Ceiling: An inventory of boundaries and interfaces only. It must not be asserted that the boundary has produced a causal selective effect.

Route two, selective effect. Conclusion level: Conditional effect. Conditions: An eligible channel-effect test (§4.4) shows that a pre-registered change to a boundary or interface has a channel effect above the threshold on the specified outcomes for cross-boundary flow, admission, or refusal. Further inferential premises: This channel-effect test. Further method requirements: Conditions for causal claims (§2.4); Retaining Competing Explanations and Residuals (§4.8.4). Can conclude: Determine the boundary’s selective effect and the distribution of cross-boundary costs for the specified channel, time window, and position. Ceiling: Limited to tested channels and outcomes. Jurisdiction and authority to act on the axis of jurisdiction and authorization must not be inferred from spatial extent, organizational scope, or scope of influence.

Can infer: Boundary selectivity; interface accessibility; cross-boundary costs.

Cannot infer: That a boundary amounts to closure; that membership status amounts to consent; that scope of influence amounts to jurisdiction.

3. Scale and Object

Nine-axis scale: Aggregation level covers individual cross-boundary events, cases of interface use, populations by membership category, distributions of admission and refusal, and aggregation rules. Spatial extent covers physical points of entry, organizational boundaries, digital interfaces, legal jurisdictions, and cross-domain channels. Time span covers the period when a boundary is in force, waiting at interfaces, migration lags, and cycles of reorganization. Organizational level covers user roles, gatekeeping teams, organizations, institutions, and governance ecologies. Causal level covers cross-boundary events, mechanisms of gatekeeping interaction, interface structure, institutional rules, and systemic conditions. Observation resolution covers original admission and refusal records, sequences of use, individual cases, flow distributions, service metrics, and summaries. Record compression loss as well. Scope of influence covers direct users, people excluded, positions where benefits or costs occur indirectly, second-order spillovers, and impacts across domains and generations. Network topology scope covers interface nodes, gatekeeping bottlenecks, alternative paths, and cross-domain connections. Scope of jurisdiction and authorization states the authorization for inclusion, exclusion, interface changes, and adjustments of rights and responsibilities separately. When moving to a larger scale, the differences must be recorded axis by axis.

Effective object: A boundary that selectively affects flows of resources, information, rights, or responsibilities.

Kept across scales: Positions inside and outside; cross-boundary channels; limits of rights and responsibilities.

Must be added when moving to a larger scale: New membership categories; interfaces across domains; representation and authorization; carryover of protections.

Changes with scale: Membership, interfaces, and the boundaries of actual control can change.

Objects to which it does not apply: Nonhuman boundaries without concepts of membership, rights, or responsibility.

Cannot be raised to: Jurisdiction arising automatically as soon as spatial extent or organizational scope expands.

4. State, Evidence, and Relations Between Variables

States: Open; selectively open; closed; disputed; reorganizing.

Observables: Membership eligibility and decisions on admission and exit; cross-boundary flows of resources, information, rights, and responsibilities; gatekeeping nodes, wait times, and reasons for refusal; differences between formal boundaries and boundaries of actual passage.

Evidence requirements: Membership lists and exceptions; records of interface use; records of cross-boundary flows and refusals.

Inputs: The candidate object, or the supported structural domain, given by the variable “Structural Domain,” together with its evidential status; roles and authorization. Route one does not require a grouping-gain test (§4.3) to pass first.

Outputs: Uptake in the sense of the action uptake layer (§7.3.5); write-back in the sense of feedback write-back (§7.3.7); exit in the sense of the protection-floor item “Genuine Exit and Alternative Uptake” (§14.8).

Time window and lag: Record lags in boundaries taking effect or changing and in exit and appeal.

Uncertainty: Record informal boundaries, access through representatives, and drift in digital spaces.

Local exclusion zone: Positions without access to the interface, without an exit, or withheld for protection.

Affected positions: Members; applicants; people excluded; people bearing burdens outside the boundary.

5. Uptake, Responsibility, Ceilings, and Correction

Uptake carriers: Membership rules; access mechanisms; legal or institutional boundaries; technical interfaces.

Responsible parties: Boundary setters; interface operators; parties granting authorization.

Normative status: Facts about a boundary are kept separate from its normative justification.

Judgment ceiling: When evidence about interfaces and impacts is sufficient, judgments may go up to the level of diagnosis.

Action ceiling: This variable produces only descriptions of boundary states, interface barriers, exclusion risks, and testing needs. It does not authorize changes to admission, exit, rights, or resource flows. Any real-world adjustment must separately pass normative bridging (§4.14.1) and have in place the normative premises stated explicitly on this occasion, itemized authorization, and operational procedures.

Counterexamples: The formal membership boundary is the opposite of the boundary of actual resource control; a digital interface is open, but physical, language, or safety barriers prevent some positions from gaining access.

Appeal: Under the common rule for appeal and restoration (§7.4), affected people inside and outside the boundary can raise objections to inclusion, exclusion, and interface barriers through a safe, accessible channel with anti-retaliation protection, and initiate a review independent of the original decision chain for the boundary.

Restoration: Under the same rule, once restoration is authorized, the designated responsible party must within the stated deadline correct membership and refusal records, actually restore affected admission rights or interface states, and reverse erroneous boundary actions. Changes leave a record, and their completion is verified.
