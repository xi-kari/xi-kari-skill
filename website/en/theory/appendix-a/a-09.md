# A.9 Structural Load (HV09) {#section-a-9}

1. Proposition and Scope of Application

Proposition: A description of structural load in human structures must state tasks, coordination losses, maintenance requirements, capacity, recovery margins, and positions bearing costs together.

Scope of application: Human structures under conditions of continuing operation, maintenance, care, or high pressure.

Conditions for suspension: Metaphors of entropy, fragility, or resilience are used without identifying tasks, capacity, and mechanisms of recovery.

2. Grounds and Limits on Conclusions

Inferential premises: None.

Method requirements: Conditions for evidence (§2.5); conditions for sources (§2.7).

Domain restriction: None.

Objects of application: None.

Support routes:

Route one, instantaneous task–capacity. Conclusion level: Descriptive classification. Conditions: Tasks or coordination requirements, capacity, recovery margins, and their distributions are observable under the same time window, position, and type mapping. Further inferential premises: None. Further method requirements: None. Can conclude: Record the instantaneous task–capacity relation, margins, backlogs, local gaps, and recovery status. Ceiling: Description within the same time window only. An instantaneous peak or gap does not automatically establish an overload mechanism, cumulative damage, or collapse.

Route two, overload mechanism. Conclusion level: Mechanism explanation. Conditions: All conditions for applying the “Load, Capacity, and Recovery” mechanism (§6.6) are met, and an eligible channel-effect test (§4.4) shows an above-threshold effect of a channel for load, replenishment, load reduction, or recovery on the preselected outcome. Further inferential premises: This channel-effect test; the “Load, Capacity, and Recovery” mechanism. Further method requirements: Conditions for causal claims (§2.4); Retaining Competing Explanations and Residuals (§4.8.4). Can conclude: Record a candidate overload or recovery mechanism within the specified position, type, time window, and channel. Ceiling: The result must not be generalized as entropy or resilience, or as inevitable collapse at every position. It does not directly generate an obligation to reduce load or make sacrifices.

Route three, historical-increment cumulative overload. Conclusion level: Intertemporal explanation. Conditions: The overload mechanism already has support from a channel-effect test and the “Load, Capacity, and Recovery” mechanism, and a historical-increment test (§4.5) shows that historical load provides a conditional increment for subsequent capacity, errors, or recovery. Further inferential premises: This channel-effect test; the “Load, Capacity, and Recovery” mechanism; this historical-increment test. Further method requirements: Conditions for causal claims; Retaining Competing Explanations and Residuals. Can conclude: Record a candidate for historical-increment cumulative damage or delayed recovery, beyond the current representation, limited to the pre-registered carrier, time window, and outcome. Ceiling: Do not infer irreversibility, inevitable collapse, attribution of responsibility, or a duty for a named subject to bear the load.

Route four, carrier cumulative overload. Conclusion level: Intertemporal explanation. Conditions: All conditions of the carrier route of the “Load, Capacity, and Recovery” mechanism (§6.6) are met; an eligible channel-effect test covers the specified channel of load, replenishment, or recovery and the stated cross-window outcome; and the carrier of current damage or capacity, accumulation or recovery across time windows, and discriminating causal materials are verified under §4.5. Further inferential premises: This channel-effect test; the “Load, Capacity, and Recovery” mechanism. Further method requirements: Conditions for causal claims; Retaining Competing Explanations and Residuals. Can conclude: Record a candidate for cumulative damage or delayed recovery through carrier transmission within the specified carrier, time window, and outcome. Ceiling: Do not determine a historical increment in the sense of a historical-increment test, or infer a damage mechanism from one overload event or a persistent correlation. Do not infer irreversibility, inevitable collapse, responsibility, or a duty to make sacrifices.

Can infer: Candidate overload, insufficient margins, maintenance gaps, and differences in recovery.

Cannot infer: That people taking up the work should continue to bear it; that high load proves devotion; that a subject under overload amounts to a mechanism of destabilization.

3. Scale and Object

Nine-axis scale: Aggregation level covers individual task loads, cases of uptake, populations of positions or groups, distributions of load and capacity, and aggregation rules. Spatial extent covers sites of work or care, organizational boundaries, digital workspaces, and the extent of outsourcing across domains. Time span covers instantaneous peaks, persistent backlogs, recovery lags, and windows across periods and generations. Organizational level covers uptake roles, teams, organizations, institutions, and governance ecologies. Causal level covers task events, mechanisms of interaction between tasks and capacity, meso-level bottleneck structures, institutional allocation, and systemic conditions. Observation resolution covers original task and working-hour records, load sequences, cases of uptake, capacity distributions, delay and error metrics, and summaries. Record compression loss as well. Scope of influence covers people directly taking up the work, people dependent on services, indirect substitutes, second-order spillovers, and costs across domains and generations. Network topology scope covers task dependencies, key bottlenecks, substitute nodes, recovery paths, and outsourcing networks across domains. Scope of jurisdiction and authorization states the authorization for task assignment, resource adjustments, stopping, use of performance evaluations, and remedy separately. The distributions of load and capacity need to be preserved.

Effective object: The structural relation of tasks and coordination requirements to available capacity and recovery margins within a given time window.

Kept across scales: Load, capacity, recovery, and positions bearing costs.

Must be added when moving to a larger scale: Load distribution; masking by aggregation; carryover of responsibility; intergenerational impacts.

Changes with scale: Bottlenecks, capacity, and ways of recovering can change with the level.

Objects to which it does not apply: Processes without sustained disequilibrium, maintenance requirements, or human uptake requirements.

Cannot be raised to: Average load masking local overload.

4. State, Evidence, and Relations Between Variables

States: Low load; bearable; critical; overloaded; recovering.

Observables: Task volume per unit of time, backlog, delays, and error rate; capacity of personnel and resources, hidden labor, and availability of substitutes; stopping, absence, exit, and recovery curves; differences between average load and its distribution at key local uptake positions.

Evidence requirements: Task volume; delays and errors; personnel and resources; recovery records; exit and absence. For the stated objective, distinguish execution, simultaneous activities, management, being on call, and opportunity costs, and state their overlaps, units, and transfers across positions, without summing them directly into a single burden. When the task in question depends on continuous blocks of time, also record how responsibilities as the default contact, being on call, and filling in at short notice cut into the usable whole blocks, without converting this into hours.

Inputs: Action uptake layer (§7.3.5); drive–uptake chain (§7.3.6); conditional potential field (§7.3.8). Route one records task volume, capacity, backlog, and recovery for the same type, time window, and position bearing the load, and does not take an established channel effect as a premise for description. For its channel effects, route two needs a channel-effect test and the “Load, Capacity, and Recovery” mechanism. For carrier accumulation, route four checks the current state, its formation, and its effects across time windows under §4.5. The additional historical conditional increment of route three is tested separately by a historical-increment test. Traces in the sense of the human-domain clause “Historical Trace Carriers” (§7.1) cannot substitute for later effects, nor for a historical-increment test. Lack of formal mechanism support for a route does not cancel general harm, continuing burdens, or historical causation already supported by independent domain evidence; each conclusion is stated separately according to its own grounds.

Outputs: State updates; behavior of destabilization; maintenance and repair needs.

Time window and lag: Distinguish instantaneous peaks, persistent backlogs, recovery lags, and intergenerational costs.

Uncertainty: Record hidden labor, outsourced costs, and absence for reasons of protection.

Local exclusion zone: Informal labor, family care, outsourcing, and uptake positions with low visibility.

Affected positions: People taking up the work; people dependent on services; people taking over; positions to which costs spill over.

5. Uptake, Responsibility, Ceilings, and Correction

Uptake carriers: Personnel; positions; procedures; facilities; budgets.

Responsible parties: Task assigners; resource allocators; parties granting authorization; parties responsible for oversight; parties responsible for remedy.

Normative status: High efficiency or the ability to carry a heavy load does not establish normative justification.

Judgment ceiling: Route one describes tasks, capacity, and recovery within the same time window. Route two uses a channel-effect test and the “Load, Capacity, and Recovery” mechanism to form a limited explanation of the channel. Route four uses §4.5 to form a judgment about cumulative damage or delayed recovery transmitted by a current carrier. Route three judges historical increments beyond the current representation only with an additional historical-increment test. Persistent correlation, a temporary overload, or an overall average cannot substitute for the corresponding evidence.

Action ceiling: This variable produces only descriptions of load, capacity, recovery, local overload, and needs to reduce load and add resources. It does not authorize task reduction, resource commitments, personnel action based on performance, or compelling people to bear the load. Any real-world adjustment must separately pass normative bridging (§4.14.1) and have in place the normative premises stated explicitly on this occasion, itemized authorization, and operational procedures.

Counterexamples: Average capacity overall is adequate, but a few key uptake positions remain overloaded; metaphors of entropy or resilience alone fail to identify tasks, capacity, or recovery channels.

Appeal: Under the common rule for appeal and restoration (§7.4), people taking up work can report hidden labor, overload, and recovery needs through a safe, accessible channel with anti-retaliation protection, and initiate a review independent of the original judgment about load or the original chain of performance or task decisions.

Restoration: Under the same rule, once restoration is authorized, the designated responsible party must within the stated deadline revoke erroneous load judgments and their effects on performance or responsibility, and actually restore task, resource, and record states. Changes leave a record, and their completion is verified.
