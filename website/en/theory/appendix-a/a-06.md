# A.6 Drive–Uptake Chain (HV06) {#section-a-6}

1. Proposition and Scope of Application

Proposition: For the chain from orientation and generation to execution, maintenance, and repayment, channels, resources, costs, responsibilities, and lags must be stated segment by segment.

Scope of application: Human collective action, projects, organizations, and institutional operations.

Conditions for suspension: Enthusiasm, vision, or commands are used in place of evidence about uptake and repayment.

2. Grounds and Limits on Conclusions

Inferential premises: None.

Method requirements: Conditions for evidence (§2.5); conditions for sources (§2.7).

Domain restriction: None.

Objects of application: None.

Support routes:

Route one, local chain segments. Conclusion level: Descriptive classification. Conditions: The inputs, outputs, delay, loss, interruptions, resources, costs, and boundaries of at least one chain segment can be observed. Further inferential premises: None. Further method requirements: None. Can conclude: Record local chain segments, missing bridges, time lags, losses, points of interruption, and positions bearing costs. Ceiling: A complete chain or an effective channel must not be claimed on the basis of a single segment or of claims about drive.

Route two, complete candidate chain. Conclusion level: Descriptive classification. Conditions: The records of the three variables orientation anchor (§7.3.3), generative node (§7.3.4), and action uptake layer (§7.3.5) can be linked segment by segment in a common mapping of object, scale, time window, and quantities. Further inferential premises: Orientation anchor; generative node; action uptake layer. Further method requirements: None. Can conclude: Record the complete candidate drive–uptake chain and evidence coverage for each segment. Ceiling: Composition of a candidate chain only. Complete records for the three variables do not establish that each segment has a causal effect.

Route three, effective channel. Conclusion level: Mechanism explanation. Conditions: The complete candidate chain has been assembled, an eligible channel-effect test (§4.4) supports the specified channel, and correspondence within the same case, shared scope of support, joint response, temporal order, capacity, and the endpoint outcome suffice to support the whole-chain effect in question (§2.4.2). Multiplying the average effects of the segments does not suffice. Further inferential premises: Orientation anchor; generative node; action uptake layer; this channel-effect test. Further method requirements: Conditions for causal claims (§2.4); Retaining Competing Explanations and Residuals (§4.8.4). Can conclude: Determine the effective drive–uptake channel, and the mechanisms of loss and interruption, within the tested segments and time window. Ceiling: Intertemporal reproduction, responsibility, normative justification, or authorization to act must not be inferred from one pass through the chain.

Can infer: Bottlenecks in the chain; disconnection between drive and uptake; hidden repayment.

Cannot infer: That strong drive amounts to sustainability; that failure is due to lack of willingness; that those taking up the work should fill gaps on their own.

3. Scale and Object

Nine-axis scale: Aggregation level covers events at individual links, cases involving chains, the population of similar chains, distributions of inputs and outputs, and aggregation rules. Spatial extent covers sites of action, project or organizational boundaries, digital spaces for collaboration, and spillovers across domains. Time span covers windows for initiation, maintenance, interruption, and recovery, and cycles across periods. Organizational level covers initiating roles, execution teams, organizations, institutions, and governance ecologies. Causal level covers events at links, mechanisms of interaction among drive, resources, and tasks, meso-level uptake chains, institutional arrangements, and systemic conditions. Observation resolution covers original records of tasks and resources, sequences of links, cases involving chains, loss distributions, performance metrics, and summaries. Record compression loss as well. Scope of influence covers positions of direct initiation and uptake, positions of indirect benefit or cost, second-order spillovers, and impacts across domains and generations. Network topology scope covers chains of dependency, alternative paths, bottlenecks, feedback connections, and bridges across domains. Scope of jurisdiction and authorization states the authorization for adopting goals, assigning tasks, committing resources, stopping, and trials separately. The chain’s position on each axis needs to be marked segment by segment.

Effective object: A traceable chain that converts orientation and generation into sustained action.

Kept across scales: The chain of orientation, generation, uptake, resources, costs, and responsibility.

Must be added when moving to a larger scale: Bridges across levels; aggregation loss; carryover of responsibility; the protection floor (§14.8).

Changes with scale: Nodes, channels, and bottlenecks can change with organizational scale.

Objects to which it does not apply: Nonhuman processes without intentional drive or human uptake.

Cannot be raised to: Local drive or a single node directly representing the complete chain.

4. State, Evidence, and Relations Between Variables

States: Connected end to end; lagging; overloaded; disrupted; replaced.

Observables: Records of the anchor being translated into tasks, budgets, rules, or schedules; inputs, outputs, delay, loss, and points of interruption for each segment; changes in the capacity of people taking up the work, in stopping, and in alternative paths; the actual contribution of chain outputs to the target outcome.

Evidence requirements: Resource flows; task and maintenance records; repayment and costs; time lags.

Inputs: Orientation anchor; generative node; action uptake layer.

Outputs: Action outcomes; load; feedback and traces of evolution.

Time window and lag: State lags in initiation, transmission, maintenance, and repayment segment by segment.

Uncertainty: Record breaks, alternative channels, and repayment outside the boundary.

Local exclusion zone: Informal, low-visibility, and cross-organizational uptake positions.

Affected positions: Initiators; people taking up the work; beneficiaries; cost bearers.

5. Uptake, Responsibility, Ceilings, and Correction

Uptake carriers: Personnel and positions; procedures; budgets; infrastructure.

Responsible parties: Those responsible at each node for conduct, decisions, authorization, and oversight, those responsible as beneficiaries, and those responsible for remedy.

Normative status: Effectiveness of the chain does not establish that its goal is normatively justified.

Judgment ceiling: When evidence for the whole chain is sufficient, judgments may go up to the level of explanation or diagnosis.

Action ceiling: This variable produces only descriptions of chain connectivity, time lags, losses, interruptions, costs, and uptake needs. It does not authorize load reduction, resource adjustments, or trials. Any real-world adjustment must separately pass normative bridging (§4.14.1) and have in place the normative premises stated explicitly on this occasion, itemized authorization, and operational procedures.

Counterexamples: A compelling vision and concentrated mobilization lack sustained resources, maintenance, or repayment; a chain that appears connected end to end shifts key costs to people taking up work outside its boundary.

Appeal: Under the common rule for appeal and restoration (§7.4), people taking up work along the chain or at affected positions can raise objections to resources, costs, and attribution to the chain through a safe, accessible channel with anti-retaliation protection, and initiate a review independent of the original judgment about the chain or the original decision chain.

Restoration: Under the same rule, once restoration is authorized, the designated responsible party must within the stated deadline actually revoke attribution to the chain as a whole and its downstream effect, and restore descriptions at the level of individual nodes and the status “unresolved.” Changes leave a record, and their completion is verified.
