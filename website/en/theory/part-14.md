# Part 14 Normative Choice {#part-title-14}

The preceding structural grammar, four empirical questions, scale transformations, mechanisms of operation, and human variables address “how objects exist, operate, and change, and what grounds we have for explaining them in these ways.” This Part asks a different question. Among several possible directions, who may choose, on what grounds, whom does the choice bind, and how do we stop and repair if it fails?

The two kinds of question must be kept separate. An explanation can be highly reliable and still be insufficient to confer authority to act. A goal can receive wide support and still breach rights floors, shift costs to low-power positions, or exceed the decision maker’s jurisdiction. Normative choice is therefore not a passage appended to diagnosis. It is a separate layer of conditions between explanation and real-world action.

The requirements in this Part belong to three levels, each with a different scope.

The first level consists of principles of argument. They state what a normative judgment must distinguish: facts and what ought to be; the value premises used, and the conflicts among them; beneficiaries and those who bear the costs; and, where options are chosen, the options, no action among them. Analytical recommendations for deliberation are bound by them as well.

The second level consists of substantive safeguards. They state what real-world adoption must actually have: first, valid authorization covering the action; second, the substance of the protections referred to by the ten items of the protection floor (§14.8), including identification of affected positions and low-power positions, safe submission and anti-retaliation, genuine refusal and exit, accessible appeal and independent review, adding evidence and correcting records, restoration and remedy, and action ceilings and expiry.

The third level is the record format. It states how this framework records the above for its own “authorized” status (§14.7), for example by pointing to the no-action option with a unique identifier and by binding exactly ten items of the protection floor. Other practices that carry equivalent responsibilities for protection, tracing, and review may adopt different formats. Whether they are equivalent must be confirmed by independent review, not claimed by the party that adopts the practice.

The test of whether a requirement belongs to the record format is this: if deleting it would change who is protected, who bears the costs, who has the right to decide, who can stop, or who can correct errors, it does not belong to the record format.

## 14.1 Facts Do Not Entail What Ought to Be Done {#section-14-1}

Factual propositions describe “what is.” Normative premises state “what is worth pursuing, protecting, or prohibiting.” Claims that “a structure is stable,” “an option is more efficient,” “the system retains a certain type,” or “a risk is rising” cannot directly entail “therefore this should be done.” Facts have a defined role in choice. They determine whether an option is possible, what its consequences might be, and the extent of risk and uncertainty. They do not choose values or create authorization on their own.

Any process that moves from explanation or diagnosis to real-world action must therefore go through the four-step operational procedure (§14.6), one step after another. The first step, “Fixing the Facts and Evidence,” fixes in advance the facts, evidence, scale, and uncertainty to be used. The second step, “Stating the Normative Premises and Conflicts,” states the normative premises actually adopted this time (§14.3), conflicts of value, beneficiaries, and those who bear the costs. The third step, “Reviewing Authorization and Comparing Options,” compares options including no action and reviews the scope of authorization, authorization, jurisdiction, validity period, and conflicts of interest. A separate normative choice record then fixes the decision. Only at the fourth step, “Bounded Execution and Correction,” can the approved option be turned into bounded execution that is reversible, monitorable, stoppable, open to appeal and restoration, and ends on expiry.

The severity of a diagnosis provides no direct route to action. High model confidence provides no route to broader permission. Stronger evidence can raise the ceiling for descriptive judgments, but cannot raise the action ceiling by itself.

Among the twelve conclusion rules, the conclusion rule “Normative Bridging” (§4.14.1) checks whether the bridge from description to real-world choice is valid, but it does not choose values. It checks only whether the descriptive conclusion, current evidence, explicit normative premises, conflicts of value, the protection floor, the scope of authorization, options, no action, the four-step operational procedure, the action ceiling, and conditions for correction all meet their requirements at the same time. Least harm and proportionality (§14.4) must be explicitly selected, explained, and reviewed in the specific choice record. They cannot be left implicit in the rule “Normative Bridging.”

## 14.2 Three Distinct Processes of Selection and Choice {#section-14-2}

Selection and choice cover at least three structurally distinct processes: systemic selection, decision-making by a subject, and collective governance. Conflating them personifies natural or distributed processes, expands personal preferences into public power, or reduces public procedures to the will of those with power.

Systemic selection has no unified deciding subject. It refers to variation or differences producing different outcomes through environments, feedback, competition, replication, and retention mechanisms. Examples include natural selection, differential retention in markets, and content spreading as a result of algorithmic rewards. An analysis of systemic selection needs to state the unit of selection, environment, retention mechanism, time window, and stochastic null model (for the null model, see §3.1). One may say that a form is retained, replicated, or eliminated. One must not say that the system “considers it better.” Retention by itself does not establish normative justification, nor do survival, stability, popularity, or efficiency. The action ceiling for systemic selection is a description of the selection process. Its choice record cannot enter the “authorized” status (§14.7), and its options cannot include an external-action option.

Decision-making by a subject is constrained. The deciding subject must be identifiable and can decide only within their own permissions, genuine options, territory, and validity period. An apparent “choice” must not be recorded as free consent if it arises from coercion, lock-in through defaults, deprivation of information, insufficient capability, unsafe refusal, or blocked exit. Failure to exit does not amount to consent. Entering a process does not amount to continuing consent. One subject’s choice does not automatically bind others.

Collective governance is governance authorized through a procedure. It addresses common affairs. The fact that they are common does not establish that the majority can represent everyone or that the scope of impact equals the scope of jurisdiction. A governance decision must make it possible to trace its boundaries, how the agenda was formed, who has authority to represent, which positions can participate, how dissent is preserved, how minorities are protected, how conflicts of interest are handled, who executes and reviews it, and when authorization expires. Numbers, loudness, efficiency, claims to represent, and legal form cannot by themselves substitute for bounded public authorization.

The three types have strict boundaries. Systemic selection cannot acquire intent or normative justification by borrowing the language that describes subjects. Decision-making by a subject cannot decide for others beyond the subject’s own permissions. Collective governance cannot bypass procedure and present governance as an enlarged version of personal choice.

## 14.3 The Five Normative Premises: Stating in Each Choice Which Are Adopted (N1–N5) {#section-14-3}

The five normative premises do not operate automatically as background assumptions. Each choice record must publicly list which of them are actually adopted for this choice and say whether each serves as an objective, a constraint, or a veto condition. A normative premise that has not been stated must not appear silently in the conclusion.

The first premise, “Explanation Does Not Confer Authority to Act,” vetoes any direct move from facts to action. On its own, this premise can yield only no action, a need for more evidence, or continued deliberation. It cannot generate an affirmative proposal.

The second premise, “Protection Must Not Be Downgraded,” requires scale elevation and action to maintain or strengthen existing protections at low-power positions. If the infrastructure that protection needs is missing, it is the action that is narrowed, not the protection.

The third premise, “High-Impact Judgments Must Be Correctable,” requires genuine access to appeal, independent review, stopping, withdrawal, restoration, remedy, and write-back to the authoritative version.

The fourth premise, “Love and Undertaking Must Not Be Conscripted,” prohibits invoking love, responsibility, care, or the persistence of a system to demand unilateral, unlimited sacrifice without an option to exit.

Bounded responsibility under the fourth premise precludes demands made in the name of love, care, or system persistence for unilateral, unlimited sacrifice without an option to exit. It does not cancel bounded commitments, obligations of support, contractual duties, or remedial responsibilities that have independent grounds. Ending or transferring a responsibility should specify who will actually take over for dependents, for how long, and with what remaining risks. This requirement must not be used to force those harmed to remain exposed to harm. Capability, causal contribution, benefit, fault, and duties of remedy should be justified separately. 〔Studies 06.06, 09.02, 10.07, 21.06, 23.01–23.07〕

The fifth premise, “Persistence and Systemic Selection Do Not Establish Normative Justification,” limits stability, efficiency, coherence, and outcomes of systemic selection to evidence about consequences. They must not substitute for a judgment of normative justification.

Normative premises in combination can conflict. For example, quickly limiting losses can conflict with full participation. Expanding public benefits can entail risks to the rights of those in minority positions. A short reversible trial can conflict with a long-term goal of stability. Conflicts cannot be smoothed out with a total score. Each conflict must be recorded separately and be traceable, with a list of the conflicting premises, the affected positions, the objections, the decision rule, and the current status. An unresolved substantive conflict can lead to suspension, a narrower trial, or retention of no action. Deleting objections cannot “resolve” it. When every feasible option (including suspension and no action) leaves unmet legitimate claims or irreversible losses, see §14.9.1.

The rights floor consists of the items of the protection floor actually in force for the current choice (for the ten items of the protection floor, see §14.8). It sets a boundary each option must pass first. It must not be treated as a preference freely exchangeable for efficiency. The rights floor and the normative premises have distinct roles. The normative premises state the grounds for choice, and the protection floor states the conditions of protection that no grounds can override.

## 14.4 Generating Options, Consequences Across Scales, and Distribution {#section-14-4}

Choice begins by generating a set of comparable options. It does not consist of finding reasons for a preselected option. Each option needs a fixed identity, and the following are fixed for it in advance: the kind of option it is, its action type, target object, territory, validity period, reversibility, expected impacts, and distributional impacts. When an option’s action, object, territory, or validity period changes, it should be put forward again as a new version or a new option. The same option must not be quietly reshaped after review.

Each set of options must contain one and only one no-action option, and it must be possible to say clearly which one it is. The no-action option is the baseline path in which the proposed new action under consideration is not carried out. Events can still occur. Existing maintenance, statutory duties, actions already under way, and the conditions for their continuation must be recorded separately. Choosing no action does not remove existing obligations. The resources and arrangements needed to actively maintain the status quo are compared as a separate option. Use the same time window to account for natural changes, accumulating risks, opportunity costs, and distributional consequences on the baseline path. Whether intervention is necessary can be judged only if no action is included. Removing it often amounts to assuming in advance that “something must be done.”

Systemic selection has another kind of option, the system variant, which compares different outcomes under different environments or retention mechanisms. It does not let the system decide. The options for decision-making by a subject and for collective governance may include external-action options, but on entering the “authorized” status one of those external-action options must be fixed. After that, the record kept outside the choice record of the selected action (that is, the action actually to be carried out) must match this option item by item: the same option, the same subject, the same object, the same action, the same territory, and the same validity period.

Consequences must be stated across scales. For every option, use at least the nine scale axes (§5.2) to specify the aggregation level, spatial extent, time span, organizational level, causal level, observation resolution, scope of influence, network topology scope, and scope of authorization where consequences occur. An immediate local benefit cannot represent long-term system benefits. An average improvement cannot cover a local exclusion zone. Stability at a higher level cannot make up for actual losses suffered by subjects at a lower level.

The distribution ledger must record benefits, costs, risks, impacts on rights, and shifts in burdens at each position, and must state whether the power position of each position is low, medium, high, or unknown. The list of affected positions includes the positions that §14.4.1 requires to be listed in full, as well as positions affected indirectly, at second order, across domains, and across generations that are identified under that section. Among them, the positions where it is harder to speak, exit, provide evidence, or shift costs must also be marked specifically as low-power positions. Every option must state who benefits, who bears the costs, who has decision-making authority, who is unable to refuse, whether costs shift from positions with more power to those with less, and whether unobservable positions are being mistaken for absent ones.

Least harm and proportionality are two explicit principles of choice, namely the principle of least harm and the principle of proportionality. The record for each of the two must specify which principle is used and which version of it, the selected option, all options compared, evaluation dimensions, adequate reasons, the evidence relied on, the reviewer, the time of review, and the status. The principle of least harm must be applied to the current choice. It must explain which harms can be avoided across all feasible options and the no-action option, and why the selected option is the narrowest. The principle of proportionality must also be applied to the current choice. It checks suitability, necessity, benefits and the costs imposed on rights, intensity, scope, and duration separately. Neither principle is a synonym for a “good objective.” Neither can be reduced to a slogan that cannot be reviewed or to the bare name of a principle.

When the two principles are applied to the current choice, the evidence relied on must be traceable within the evidence actually used this time. The reviewer must be distinct from the deciding subject and must be a specific person who bears responsibility for independent review. Review must not come later than the option’s entry into the “authorized” status. Only the current version of a principle can be used. Expiry, the validity of the ten items of the protection floor, and the timing of further review must all cover the validity period of the selected option. An expired review record cannot be used to keep the “authorized” status in place.

### 14.4.1 The Scope and Limits of the Responsibility to Identify {#section-14-4-1}

Impacts that are indirect, second-order, cross-domain, or intergenerational have no natural endpoint, so identifying them is a responsibility proportionate to risk. Directly affected positions, low-power positions that are known or have been pointed out by others, and positions that application modules require to be listed separately must all be listed in full; the proportionate standard below does not apply to them. For other positions, identification traces outward along the channels already stated and the nine scale axes. The more severe, irreversible, widespread, or likely to accumulate the possible harm is, and the lower the power position of those possibly affected, the farther the tracing should go. Tracing may stop when continuing it can no longer reasonably change the judgment, the comparison of options, or the protective arrangements. Channels not yet stated that others point out must be added to the tracing, or reasons for not adopting them must be stated. This limit is recorded together with the channels already searched, the positions consulted, and what remains unknown, so that others can challenge it.

Unknowns are handled in two ways. The first kind is an unknown for which there are reasonable grounds to think it may reveal a failure of the protection floor, or that is sufficient to change the comparison of options (including least harm, proportionality, and distribution). Such an unknown blocks the part of adoption it affects until evidence is added, the option is narrowed, or protection with separate grounds is in place. All other unknowns are the second kind. They are recorded as they are, with conditions set for observation and renewed identification. Positions identified only after the fact enjoy the same appeal, correction, and remedy and need not first prove that they could have been foreseen.

The limit of identification, the division between direct and indirect, and the kind to which an unknown is assigned cannot be decided solely by a party that benefits from a narrower limit. Their grounds are published in advance and open to appeal, and they are referred to independent review in high-impact cases or when there is a conflict of interest. The same responsibility applies to no action and to maintaining the status quo. Those harmed under the status quo are not exempt from identification because the harm has long existed. The proportionate standard applies only to identifying indirect and more distant positions. It does not reduce the protection that identified positions receive under the ten items of the protection floor, nor can it postpone protection against known harm on the grounds that identification cannot be exhaustive.

## 14.5 Coercion, Defaults, Agendas, Representation, and Minority Protection {#section-14-5}

Before a subject says “yes,” check the conditions under which they can say “no.” Both decision-making by a subject and collective governance must check the conditions of coercion and choice item by item and keep a record of the check. It examines whether direct or indirect coercion exists, whether the default locks in the result through inertia, complexity, or penalties, whether information is understandable and symmetric, whether the subject has decision-making capability, whether refusal is safe, and whether exit is genuinely feasible. Each check must give a status, rationale, and the evidence relied on. A status of “unknown” does not count as passing. An item recorded as “not applicable” must also state why it genuinely does not apply.

Default design is not a neutral background condition. Who sets the default, how much switching costs, and how silence is interpreted can all change the distribution of choices. Coercion also takes forms beyond commands. Tying access to essential resources to compliance, retaliation through relationships, identity exposure, withholding information, unaffordable exit costs, or moralizing care responsibilities can turn formal options into a single path.

The evidential weight of consent varies with conditions. Not exiting does not by itself amount to continuing consent. If an initial choice also changed the conditions of later choices, for example through interrupted skills, housing or services tied to relationships, debt, or dependence on care, “continuing to stay” provides weaker evidence. Two things should be checked separately: the conditions under which consent was originally given, and the conditions under which refusal, negotiation, and exit are still possible now. This does not cancel bounded commitments and obligations of support that have independent grounds; their ending and transfer are handled under the normative premise “Love and Undertaking Must Not Be Conscripted” (§14.3). Nor does it require every future choice to remain open forever. The refusal, stopping, and exit referred to by the protection-floor items “Genuinely Effective Refusal and Stopping” and “Genuine Exit and Alternative Uptake” (§14.8), however, are not closed by past consent.

Collective governance must also check its governance procedure item by item and keep a record of the check. Agenda formation checks who can raise questions, who can exclude options, and when emergency procedures end. Representation checks the source and scope of authorization, whether it can be withdrawn, and who is unrepresented. Participation checks safety, accessibility, information, and actual capability to influence decisions. Minority protection checks rights that majority decisions cannot cancel, local exclusion zones, and conditions for suspension. The conflict-of-interest check asks whether the people who decide, execute, evaluate, and benefit are the same. Accountability for execution checks whether responsible parties can be reviewed, stopped, and replaced.

Responding to specific views and allowing rules to change are different capabilities. Whether it is feasible to veto a proposal, propose an alternative, amend agenda rules, or replace those who hear a matter or make the final decision must be stated separately. When responses are possible only within a set topic while the agenda and the final decision cannot be challenged, record truthfully that “the rule layer cannot be changed.” Do not record this as an absence of feedback, nor as adequate capacity for correction already being in place. Recording it in this way does not substitute for the conditions for correction. In high-impact cases or when there is a conflict of interest, if the final decision cannot be challenged and there is no external independent review, neither the normative premise “High-Impact Judgments Must Be Correctable” (§14.3) nor the protection-floor item “External Independent Review” (§14.8) counts as satisfied. What evidence or dispute over rights is sufficient to trigger review at the rule layer should be published in advance.

Formal participation does not amount to genuine participation. A public meeting, a complaint channel, or a majority vote cannot by itself establish that people in low-power positions can safely express views and influence the decision. Each objection must be preserved separately and be traceable, together with its claim, the position it comes from, evidence, reasons for how it was handled, deadline for response, and safe submission channel. Within the framework, substantive objections capable of changing key facts, minimum protections, option feasibility, or valid permissions must be addressed before entering the “authorized” status. Suspend the affected part if the reasons raised have not received a response suited to them. Whether an objection is decisive in this way is judged by criteria published in advance and open to appeal, and the question is referred to independent review when there is a conflict of interest. Objections must not be excluded because few people raise them, because a majority deems them “unimportant,” or because they are inconvenient to handle. When differences in values persist after a reasoned response, preserve the minority view under an explicit decision rule that meets the requirements. Passing does not require fabricating unanimity.

Within the framework, “authorized” is an internal status that marks the scope of use the framework endorses for itself. It does not substitute for legal validity under real-world institutions, official powers, or consent of those concerned. The procedures above cannot override matters that applicable law or the protection floor makes nonwaivable. A procedure record cannot supply a legitimate decision rule where none exists. Emergencies require separately specified temporary authority, the narrowest scope, expiry, and subsequent review. “Objections have been answered” does not confer emergency power. Three situations should be recorded separately: the existence of opposition, reasons for opposition that remain unaddressed, and failure of protection conditions. 〔Studies 14.06, 19.02, 21.04, 25.02〕

## 14.6 Scope of Authorization, the Four-Step Operational Procedure, and Bounded Authorization (J Axis, O1–O4) {#section-14-6}

The scope of authorization specifies who may decide, on what source of authority, for which object and action, and within which territory and validity period. Authorization must hold item by item. One and the same valid authorization must cover the deciding subject, target object, single action, territory, and entire validity period at once; this is called itemized authorization. One authorization covering the subject and another covering the action cannot be combined into a single authorization. An increase in the scope of observation, scope of influence, or technical capability cannot be taken to expand jurisdiction.

The choice record must fix the source, scope, and validity period of authorization. Each option’s territory must equal that scope, and its validity period must fall within that period. To proceed to external action, the choice record, the selected option, the record of the selected action, and that itemized authorization must match item by item. The existence of a source of authorization does not establish legal validity. Legal validity does not establish normative justification. These must be stated separately in the third step of the four-step operational procedure, “Reviewing Authorization and Comparing Options.”

The four-step operational procedure is not an optional appendix. The first step, “Fixing the Facts and Evidence,” prevents facts, thresholds, and evidence levels from being changed after seeing the options. The second step, “Stating the Normative Premises and Conflicts,” keeps value premises, those who bear the costs, and conflicts visible. The third step, “Reviewing Authorization and Comparing Options,” prevents omission of no action, conflicts of interest, and the limits of authorization. The fourth step, “Bounded Execution and Correction,” prevents a bounded decision from expanding during execution into an indefinite, irreversible action for which no one is responsible. If any step is missing, the choice record can go no further than the “under review” or “suspended” status (§14.7).

Entering the “authorized” status also requires the following. The normative premises adopted are all stated on record and can be found, and they do not consist only of “Explanation Does Not Confer Authority to Act” (§14.3). Conflicts of value and objections have been recorded. Each of the ten items of the protection floor passes. Affected positions and low-power positions have been listed and identified under §14.4.1, and the limit of identification has been recorded. The selected option is a feasible external-action option. Least harm and proportionality have been explicitly selected (§14.4). A valid itemized authorization covers this action. The records of the four steps of the operational procedure correspond to one another and follow on from one another. Stopping, appeal, independent review, restoration, remedy, and lapse on expiry are all actually capable of changing the status.

Itemized authorization specifies sufficient coverage for a particular action. It does not deny that actual institutions consist of multiple actions, each with its own authority. Intake, adjudication, preservation measures, service of process, and enforcement may be carried out in succession by different subjects under the law. Check each action’s own authorization and prerequisites separately, then explain how the preceding result serves as valid input to the next step. Errors in both directions need to be avoided. Several authorizations, each missing a piece, cannot be combined to create authority for the same action. Nor does the fact that no one person holds all permissions invalidate an entire lawful procedure with multiple steps.

Personal consent, private appointment, public authority, and an organization’s legally established standing to make a request can also be valid separately. When a public action is lawful without a private appointment, that does not establish personal consent or permit expansion into other matters. The framework’s internal issuance status determines only the scope of use it is willing to certify. The legal effect of external procedures, normative disputes, and actual effects of protection must still be assessed separately on their respective evidence.

## 14.7 Status, Action Ceilings, and the Correction Loop {#section-14-7}

A choice record has only seven statuses: draft, under review, authorized, suspended, stopped, restored, and closed. Every change of status must append to the record the previous status, the time, the responsible party, and the grounds. It must not overwrite the existing history.

The “draft” status permits organizing materials and comparing options within existing valid permissions. It grants no new authority for real-world intervention.

“Under review” means that the first three steps of the four-step operational procedure (§14.6), normative conflicts, protection, or authorization are still under review. A need for preservation measures may be raised at this stage, but actual protection, freezing of resources, collection of further evidence, or reversible trials still require applicable, independently valid authorization. Actions under existing statutory duties or emergency powers are recorded within their own scope. They acquire no additional permissions from the “under review” status.

Only decision-making by a subject and collective governance can enter the “authorized” status, and the action ceiling covers only the specific option selected and authorized.

Enter the “suspended” status when a key fact, protection, authorization, procedure, or monitoring condition ceases to hold, and stop the part of implementation affected by that failure. The “under review” or “suspended” status by itself does not authorize freezing resources, restricting access, collecting information, or continuing intervention. Action needed to avoid immediate harm requires a separately valid duty or authorization, a specified scope, and conditions for stopping and review.

Enter the “stopped” status when a condition for stopping is triggered, authorization is withdrawn, the validity period expires, or a prohibited action is taken. Terminate the affected actions under the applicable stopping procedures, and verify safe wind-down and any remedial duties that remain independently valid. Do not wait for new theoretical grounds.

Enter the “restored” status after the recovery set in advance has been completed and verifiable remedy and write-back to the authoritative version have been initiated.

The “closed” status can be entered only when outstanding harm, appeals, remedies, and responsibilities for records have been settled or formally handed off. Any later new action requires a new record.

For each action, its maximum level, prohibited actions, conditions that trigger stopping, and the responsible party with authority to stop it must be stated in advance. The final action ceiling is the narrowest intersection of the following: the descriptive ceiling, normative principles, the protection floor, the selected option, that itemized authorization, the trial scope set by the fourth step, “Bounded Execution and Correction,” reversibility, and expiry. Tightening any item tightens the overall ceiling. A failure or unknown in any item cannot be offset by “high scores” elsewhere.

Appeal must be accessible, have a time limit, and be capable of changing a record or an action. It must lead to external independent review in high-impact cases or when there is a conflict of interest. Anti-retaliation mechanisms protect submitting evidence, raising objections, refusal, stopping, appeal, and exit. Restoration goes beyond withdrawing an order. It includes returning to an acceptable state, correction of authoritative records, synchronization of downstream versions, remedy of real harm, and tracking of residual harm. Every authorization has an expiry time and lapses automatically at expiry. Continued execution must go through authorization again rather than being renewed by default.

Correction has four distinct endpoints. Views are heard safely. Relevant evidence is understood. An authority with jurisdiction actually changes the erroneous decision. Restored resources and rights reach the people concerned. An earlier step can have independent value without closing a later one. A higher rate of overturned decisions is not automatically better. One should check whether substantiated errors can be corrected, who could not enter the process, and what actually reaches people after correction. A record of restoration cannot erase harm that occurred, opportunities that were missed, or costs incurred by others. 〔Studies 21.04, 21.08, 28.04〕

## 14.8 The Ten Items of the Protection Floor and How They Connect to the Choice Record (PF-1–PF-10) {#section-14-8}

The ten items of the protection floor always remain one complete whole. Scale elevation, object aggregation, option comparison, and convenience of execution do not reduce it. A choice record must account for each of the ten items in turn, with none missing and none repeated. Each item states its applicability status, rationale, evidence, mechanism, responsible party, and conditions that trigger stopping, together with the arrangements for appeal, review, restoration, remedy, and expiry. The rights floor that the record states must be exactly the same as the items among the ten that are currently “in force.” A general statement that “protection has been considered” is insufficient.

The mechanism on which each protection relies must be a mechanism actually stated in the same record, namely one of safe submission, anti-retaliation, appeal, independent review, restoration, remedy, tracking of residual harm, or write-back to the authoritative version. The responsible party for the protection must match the owner of that mechanism. A mechanism that has a name but no content, a mechanism that cannot be found in the record, and independence that is merely claimed do not count as protection. Items of protection specific to humans may be recorded as “not applicable” only if the enclosing object and the downstream use together establish that the impacts are exclusively nonhuman. If the enclosing context involves effects on humans, sentient subjects, mixed objects, rights, or resources, rewriting the scope of impact as “nonhuman only” does not lower the protection floor.

The ten items, in order, are as follows.

Item one: Listing Affected Positions and Low-Power Positions. The affected positions and low-power positions listed and identified under §14.4.1 are entered in the options and the distribution ledger.

Item two: Low-Exposure Channels for Submission. It provides anonymous submission, submission through a representative, or low-exposure submission, so that a high-risk channel requiring a real name is not the only route.

Item three: Genuinely Effective Refusal and Stopping. Refusal and stopping are genuinely effective within the process. Past consent does not amount to permanent consent.

Item four: Effective Appeal. It provides appeal with a time limit and the ability to change the outcome.

Item five: Anti-Retaliation. It protects submitting evidence, raising objections, refusal, stopping, appeal, and exit against retaliation.

Item six: Adding Evidence and Correcting Records. It allows evidence to be added, disputes to be marked, source records to be corrected, and downstream versions to be synchronized.

Item seven: External Independent Review. It provides external independent review when impacts are high, conflicts of interest exist, or internal correction fails.

Item eight: Actual Capability for Withdrawal, Restoration, and Repair. Withdrawal, restoration, repair, and write-back to the authoritative version all need to exist as actual capabilities.

Item nine: Genuine Exit and Alternative Uptake. It provides genuine exit and transfer. Where exit is impossible, it provides representation and alternative arrangements to take over.

Item ten: Action Ceiling and Expiry. It fixes in advance the action ceiling, prohibited actions, conditions that trigger stopping, the responsible party, and the time of review.

When the objects affected are humans, sentient subjects, mixed objects, or unknown objects, all ten items of the protection floor must be “in force.” Any item that is “pending,” “failed,” or “not applicable” blocks entry into the “authorized” status. Mechanisms specific to humans may be recorded as “not applicable,” with reasons, only when the evidence limits the object and downstream use to purely nonhuman processes. The tenth item, “Action Ceiling and Expiry,” and the requirements that real-world action must meet for the scope of authorization, responsibility, stopping, review, restoration, and expiry, however, always apply. When the use changes, it must be reviewed again. The earlier “not applicable” cannot carry over.

The protection floor cannot be lowered. Moving from a local to a wider scale, from the short to the long term, or from a single relation to an institution or platform can only preserve or strengthen protection. If a wider scale hides low-power positions, restore the local record, narrow the action, and strengthen protection. Aggregate benefits must not cancel local rights. Preserving or strengthening applies to these protections themselves, including the protective arrangements that low-power positions actually enjoyed before the action. It does not guarantee that no position’s outcomes in resources, opportunities, or life will worsen. When it is disputed whether an existing arrangement is a protection or an outcome, treat it first as a protection. Whether losses in outcomes are acceptable is argued separately through the normative premises and the least harm and proportionality records under §14.4. Burden shifts falling on low-power positions or positions unable to exit must be listed position by position and must not be offset by aggregate or average benefits (§14.9). When every feasible option leaves unmet legitimate claims or irreversible losses, record this under §14.9.1.

How the world “ought to choose” cannot be reduced to a single objective function. The framework provides a structure for tracing a choice. It first distinguishes systemic selection, decision-making by a subject, and collective governance. It then sets out facts, values, rights, options, consequences, distribution, authorization, and procedure separately. The narrowest action ceiling, execution that can be stopped, and actual repair keep any decision bounded, open to challenge and correction, and subject to expiry.

The protection floor provides minimum procedural requirements and limits on burdens. It does not exhaust substantive rights. For each matter, still specify the relevant rights, claimants, duty bearers, applicable grounds, conflicts, and remedies. The ten mechanisms do not automatically establish that a distribution of income, eligibility, care, or property is justified. Conversely, a right with independent grounds does not come into existence only when the framework’s forms are used.

## 14.9 Distribution Across Circles and Responsibility for Inaction {#section-14-9}

Options involving multiple circles must state local benefits, cross-boundary spillovers, and costs in different time windows separately. Better metrics in the primary circle cannot offset harm to rights in another circle. An overall average cannot cover people unable to exit. People in positions where memberships overlap may bear costs more than once. Bridging parties may bear translation costs and risk of conflict. Responsibilities and histories can remain after a temporary circle dissolves.

No action is also an option. State which rules and allocations of resources it retains, who continues to benefit or suffer harm, which clocks keep advancing, when to reopen evaluation, and who is responsible for observation and protection. If there is ongoing harm, a statutory duty, irreversible lock-in, or a position from which exit is impossible, “waiting for more evidence” cannot be extended indefinitely. Even without the main action, minimal protection, appeal, disclosure, or temporary uptake may be needed.

The prediction layer cannot compare options automatically. Foresight provides conditional consequences, the normative layer discloses conflicts of value and the protection floor, and the authorization layer determines who may act. Predictive accuracy, complexity, and technical capability do not confer a qualification to rule.

### 14.9.1 Bounded Judgment When Every Option Involves Losses {#section-14-9-1}

Sometimes, within the available resources and time, every feasible option, including no action, leaves unmet legitimate claims or irreversible losses. Suspension and waiting then have costs as well (§12.14 and §14.9), but the fact that waiting has costs does not generate authorization to act (§13.6). In such cases, do not invent an option with zero cost, and do not stop at a list of questions on the grounds that the conflict remains unresolved. Within what the evidence and explicit normative premises allow, the analysis should still give a reasoned, bounded judgment for deliberation. It is an analytical recommendation and does not decide on behalf of qualified subjects (§12.10).

The judgment needs to state the recommended option and the normative premises it relies on; what the decision rule in the conflict record is, who proposed it, and who must adopt it (§14.3); who bears the unmet claims or losses, and why those claims still hold; the responsibilities that continue afterward and the responsible parties for them, including mitigation, remedy, explanation, and review; and what changes in evidence, resources, or authority would change the judgment, and when to reassess. A decision rule is a normative position that those who propose and adopt it must publicly own, for example not treating one person’s life or basic safety merely as a replaceable input into others’ aggregate benefit. Making it public does not make it qualified; whether it qualifies is judged under §14.5. When the rule itself is disputed, the analysis presents the judgments under different rules side by side and does not select one on behalf of qualified subjects.

Three limits apply together.

First, before claiming there is no alternative, one must show that the constraints are real: who controls the scarcity, and whether it comes from a prior decision that can itself be questioned (§2.14); whether the option set was generated under §12.9 and §14.4, and whether any option was deleted because of costs to the decision maker. When authority is insufficient, refer the matter to a subject with authority rather than treating this as grounds for having no alternative (§12.11). This showing is open to appeal and is referred to review independent of the decision maker.

Second, matters that applicable law or the protection floor makes nonwaivable do not lapse because every option involves losses. A claim that even these matters cannot all be preserved at once must state which of them conflict and which options have been ruled out and on what grounds, and review independent of the decision maker determines whether the claim holds. If it is determined not to hold, return to options that do not cross these matters. If it is determined to hold, the framework still does not endorse any option with the “authorized” status, and the protections that can still be preserved continue to apply. A subject with the corresponding duties and authority may decide only under its own statutory or emergency authority, within the narrowest scope and bearing responsibility for the decision, with an expiry time and subsequent review (§14.5 and §14.7).

Third, after a subject with authority has chosen, the claims of those harmed do not disappear as a result. The record retains these claims and how they are subsequently handled, and does not delete the costs from the ledger.

## 14.10 Resources, Standing, Representation, and Non-Domination {#section-14-10}

A normative evaluation can ask about three things at the same time: actual control of resources, equal standing within institutions, and rights over agendas and representation. An account of how these reinforce each other needs to explain whether resources make it possible to refuse dependence, whether standing changes rules of admission and evidence, and whether representatives can change budgets and execution. These can also be out of sync. Greater benefits can come with humiliating barriers to access. An identity can be affirmed while resources remain inaccessible. Representation by people with the same identity can increase while dissent within the group remains excluded. An improvement in one cannot settle the other two.

The absence of actual intervention does not guarantee the absence of domination. If one party retains the power to change another party’s key options arbitrarily, the other may have to appease them in advance, remain silent, or limit their own actions. When non-domination is adopted as the principle for an evaluation, state which powers can be constrained and how, whether decisions can be challenged, and how those imposing constraints are themselves held accountable. This normative choice cannot be inferred automatically from a low rate of conflict or present satisfaction. It does not require treating every intervention under the law as domination.

The scope of those affected, the scope of those subject to a governance structure, legal membership, and the scope of formal representation: these four need not coincide. The first two help identify overlooked problems and needs for protection. The latter two must be determined under the specific institution and authorization. None can confer all voting rights or jurisdiction merely by applying one principle. The review checks the framework itself adopts are its arrangement for its own bounded action. They do not establish the only legitimate system of decision-making for every community. How ordinary disagreements are resolved, which rights cannot be canceled by a majority, and when emergency procedures end should be stated publicly and be open to review.
