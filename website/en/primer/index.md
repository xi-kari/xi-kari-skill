# Ten sentences to start with

This page has ten sentences. They do not tell you how the world is. They help you fool yourself a little less when you reach a judgment.

All ten guard against one kind of mistake: your grounds carry you this far, and your words go further. They apply when you read the news or a report, sit in a meeting, advise someone, or decide for yourself.

The book is long and written for readers, human or AI, who need to check each step. This page is a plain summary of it, and most of the examples were chosen here to make a point easy to see. It gets you through the door and does not replace the source. Each sentence lists the sections it summarizes; cite the theory by those section numbers.

## 1. Say first what you are talking about

“Is this shop doing well?” cannot be answered as asked. Which shop? Compared with last year, or with the shop next door? Measured by turnover, profit, or returning customers? Based on the owner's word, the books, or one visit on a Saturday?

A judgment has somewhere to stand once four things are fixed: the object, the question, the period, and the material in hand. Change any one of them and the earlier conclusion has to be checked again; it cannot simply be carried over. Many arguments go nowhere because the two sides are not talking about the same thing.

Source: [§3.1](../theory/part-03#section-3-1), [§1.8](../theory/part-01#section-1-8) {.xk-refs}

## 2. Same word, same number: check that they mean the same thing

A pass rate rises from sixty to ninety per cent. The students may have learned more, or the pass mark may have been lowered. You weigh two pounds less. You may have lost weight, or you may be using a different scale.

A number can be computed exactly and still not measure what you care about. One word can name two different things in two documents, and one indicator can be calculated differently before and after. Align them before you compare. If they cannot be aligned, do not compare yet.

Source: [§2.5.1](../theory/part-02#section-2-5-1), [§3.4.1](../theory/part-03#section-3-4-1), [§5.10](../theory/part-05#section-5-10). A documented case: [Mars Climate Orbiter](../cases/mars-climate-orbiter) {.xk-refs}

## 3. One step holding does not make the next step hold

“I think this will happen, so we should do that.” Several doors are hidden in that sentence.

Making sense of what has happened is explanation. Saying what would follow under stated conditions is projection. Saying in advance what will happen is a forecast. Saying what should be done is a choice. One more door comes after these: who has the authority to do it.

Being able to explain does not mean being able to forecast. Being able to forecast does not mean something should be done. That it should be done does not mean you are entitled to do it. Each door needs grounds of its own. Seeing more accurately than others does not give you the right to decide for them.

Source: [§1.1](../theory/part-01#section-1-1), [§1.4](../theory/part-01#section-1-4), [§12.1](../theory/part-12#section-12-1). Guide: [Prediction and action](../concepts/prediction). A documented case: [Limits of democratic authorization](../cases/democratic-authorization) {.xk-refs}

## 4. Two links that each hold may still not connect

Training raises skills. People with higher skills find work more easily. So training helps people find work? Not necessarily. The people whose skills the training raised may not be the people who were out of work for lack of skills.

Every link can have evidence while the chain as a whole is broken. What matters is whether the same people, in the same matter, went through both links. Averages work the same way: a normal total does not show that every part is normal.

Source: [§1.11](../theory/part-01#section-1-11), [§2.4.2](../theory/part-02#section-2-4-2). Guide: [Local and whole-chain validity](../concepts/chain) {.xk-refs}

## 5. Whether a summary is enough depends on what you are asking

Two counters, twenty people waiting in all. If you only want to know whether today is busy, the total is enough. If you want to know whether one counter is overwhelmed, it is not: the queues may be ten and ten, or zero and twenty.

The book works this example through. Two queues of 0 to 30 people each give 961 possible cases. Judging “is either queue longer than 15?” from the average alone, even the best possible rule gets at least 85 of them wrong.

A summary is not a mistake. It is enough for some questions only. When it falls short, add the one thing that is missing, such as the length of the longer queue. You do not have to bring back every detail.

Source: [§3.4](../theory/part-03#section-3-4), [C.9](../theory/appendix-c#section-c-9). Guide: [Representation and scale](../concepts/representation). A documented case: [Hurricane Ian and the forecast cone](../cases/representation-hurricane-cone) {.xk-refs}

## 6. History matters, but first check whether it is already written into the present

The past certainly has effects. Many of them are already held in the present: savings, injuries, skills, contracts, reputation. Look closely at the present and most of the past's work is already counted. That does not mean the past did nothing. The present is how it acts.

Two people each have a hundred thousand in the bank. One saved it over ten years; the other won it last month. To guess whether each will still have it next year, the balance is not enough, and you have to ask where the money came from. But if you already know each person's income, spending and habits, “where it came from” tells you nothing more.

So bring in the past separately only where the present cannot account for what you see. Watch for the opposite move as well: describing the present very coarsely and then announcing that history decides everything.

Source: [§4.5](../theory/part-04#section-4-5), [C.11](../theory/appendix-c#section-c-11). Guide: [History and carriers](../concepts/history) {.xk-refs}

## 7. A reply is not feedback, feedback is not learning, and learning is not repair

You send a complaint and receive an acknowledgment. That shows the letter arrived.

After it arrived, was the matter handled any differently because of it? If so, it had an effect. Once the handling changed, will the same kind of matter be handled that way next time? If so, something was learned. Once it was learned, was the harm already done made good? If so, it was repaired.

Each step up needs one more layer of evidence. A single acknowledgment cannot carry you up the whole staircase.

Source: [§3.3](../theory/part-03#section-3-3), [§6.2](../theory/part-06#section-6-2), [§6.3](../theory/part-06#section-6-3), [§6.4](../theory/part-06#section-6-4). Guide: [Feedback and learning](../concepts/feedback). Documented cases: [Flint water complaints](../cases/flint-water-feedback), [Toyota andon system](../cases/toyota-andon) {.xk-refs}

## 8. Not seen is not the same as not there

No record does not show that nothing happened. Not shown to work is not the same as shown not to work. Where the material is silent, write “unknown”, and say which conclusions that unknown affects and which it does not.

When several explanations all fit, keep them all, and write down what observation would tell them apart. A story that reads smoothly has only shown that it is worth checking.

Source: [§1.4.1](../theory/part-01#section-1-4-1), [§4.8.4](../theory/part-04#section-4-8-4) {.xk-refs}

## 9. Write a forecast down first, then look at the outcome

“Relations will improve” can never be wrong, because afterwards some improvement can always be found.

So write down in advance what counts as a hit, when it will be settled, and how confident you are. Compare when the outcome is in. A miss is a miss; you cannot swap in a different question and count yourself right.

Compare against the dullest method too. “Tomorrow will be like today” and “this year will be like the same period last year” are often quite accurate. However elaborate the reasoning, a forecast that cannot beat them has shown no skill.

Source: [§12.2](../theory/part-12#section-12-2), [§12.3](../theory/part-12#section-12-3), [§12.4](../theory/part-12#section-12-4), [§12.7](../theory/part-12#section-12-7) {.xk-refs}

## 10. Facts alone do not yield “ought”

“This method is more efficient” is a fact. “So we should adopt it” still lacks several things: what we value, who bears the cost, who has the authority to decide, and how to stop or reverse if it goes wrong.

Doing nothing is also a choice. It has costs and belongs in the comparison. It is not the same as carrying on with the current practice.

Do not swing to the other extreme either. Where the grounds are enough for a conclusion, state the conclusion plainly. How strongly you put a judgment should follow the weight of the evidence, not the tone.

Source: [§14.1](../theory/part-14#section-14-1), [§12.9](../theory/part-12#section-12-9), [§1.4.3](../theory/part-01#section-1-4-3), [§1.4.4](../theory/part-01#section-1-4-4). Guides: [Choices with unavoidable losses](../concepts/choices-with-loss), [Affected positions](../concepts/affected-positions) {.xk-refs}

## What these ten sentences cannot do

They cannot stand in for knowing an industry, an illness or a period of history; that takes the specialist knowledge of each field. They do one thing: when you join what you know into a conclusion, they help you see where you stepped across a gap without noticing.

They have to be tested too. The book's own requirement is that the approach should give way if, compared with simpler methods, it does not make judgments more accurate or errors fewer. Whether it works has to be shown by records written down first and checked against outcomes afterwards, not by the length of the book or the number of its terms.

Source: [§1.3](../theory/part-01#section-1-3), [§1.9](../theory/part-01#section-1-9), [§1.12](../theory/part-01#section-1-12), [§16.16](../theory/part-16#section-16-16), [§16.21](../theory/part-16#section-16-21) {.xk-refs}
